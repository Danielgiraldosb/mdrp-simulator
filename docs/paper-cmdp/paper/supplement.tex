\section{Detailed literature review}
\label{sec:App_literature_review}

This section reviews the literature related to the three components of the problem: the operational MDRP, fairness and compensation in delivery platforms, and courier behaviour. Table~\ref{tab:Litclass} in the main body provides a systematic summary of their similarities and differences relative to our work.

\subsection{Operational block}
\label{sec:App_lit_operational}

\cite{reyes2018meal} defined the MDRP as a rolling-horizon, dynamic-deterministic problem and released the Grubhub instance library; \cite{yildiz2019provably} obtained provably high-quality solutions for a deterministic variant; \cite{steever2019dynamic} revise assignments on every event; \cite{Ulmer2021} anticipate random ready times with approximate dynamic programming; and later contributions address shared logistics \citep{bi2023meal}, courier operating regions \citep{auad2023courier}, batching for crowdsourced fleets \citep{simoni2023crowdsourced}, ghost kitchens \citep{neria2024restaurant} and on-demand delivery from stores \citep{liu2021multiperiod}. In all of these, acceptance is perfect or exogenous.

\subsection{Economic block}
\label{sec:App_lit_economic}

Most operational models pay a fixed amount per delivery or minimise distance as a cost proxy. \cite{reyes2018meal} and \cite{yildiz2019provably} include a shift-level guaranteed minimum set by the company; \cite{wen2010dynamic} balance workload and income opportunities within a multi-period routing model; \cite{lesmana2019balancing} trace the efficiency--fairness frontier of ride-sourcing; \cite{bajec2022strategic} use Shapley-value incentives; \cite{sobhanan2026equity} give equity--cost frontiers; \cite{gupta2022fairfoody}, \cite{nair2022work4food} and \cite{martinez2024exploring} bring fairness to food delivery. None integrates acceptance-dependent pay, a real tariff and an intertemporal guarantee within a rolling-horizon routing problem.

\subsection{Behavioural block}
\label{sec:App_lit_behavioural}

\cite{liao2020multi} balance workload and argue that equitable distribution is an incentive; reinforcement-learning approaches \citep{ahamed2020deep,bozanta2022courier} include availability in the state but keep acceptance exogenous; \cite{haferkamp2023heatmap} address repositioning; \cite{quintero2020computational} makes acceptance depend on distance. The fair-machine-learning literature shows that short- and long-horizon fairness diverge \citep{corbettdavies2023measure}. \citet{cheng2025realtime} train deep Q-networks for dispatching and idle-courier steering on a fixed fleet paid by the hour; the fairness of the workload is reported ex post as the standard deviation of orders and delivery time per courier, but it is neither optimised nor part of the state.

\subsection{Illustrative numerical example}
\label{sec:example}

We use a two-courier example to connect the components of Section~\ref{sec:SDP} with the dispatching decisions and to show why a policy that is fair within every window can be unfair at the end of the day. Couriers $A$ and $B$ are connected from 12:00 to 13:00, so $\conn_A(T) = \conn_B(T) = 60$ minutes whatever is dispatched; the tariff is flat at 2{,}200 COP per order; $B$ is slightly farther from the store than $A$. Four routes become available during the hour: at 12:00 a bundle of three orders from one store (pay 6{,}600 COP, duration 30 minutes including service), and at 12:30, 12:40 and 12:50 one singleton each (pay 2{,}200 COP, duration 8 minutes, so that the courier who takes one is free again before the next window). Coverage is the first priority of every policy, so all four routes are dispatched in every case; the question is to whom.

At $t = $ 12:00 the state is $S_0$ with both couriers idle and $h_A(0) = h_B(0) = (0, 0, 0)$. Both the per-window policy and the history-aware policy dispatch the bundle to $A$, the nearer courier, since nothing distinguishes the two couriers yet. At $t = $ 12:30 the state contains $h_A = (6{,}600, 3, 30)$ and $h_B = (0, 0, 0)$, that is, hourly earnings so far of $w_A(t) = 13{,}200$ COP/h and $w_B(t) = 0$, and one singleton is available. Consider two decisions. The per-window policy balances the pay offered within the window among the free couriers: with a single route, the window statistic is the same whichever courier receives it (one courier is offered 2{,}200 and the other 0), so the tie is broken by distance and the singleton goes to $A$. The same happens at 12:40 and 12:50. The end-of-day hourly earnings are $w_A = 6{,}600 + 3 \times 2{,}200 = 13{,}200$ COP/h and $w_B = 0$, and by Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}
\begin{equation*}
\FG = \frac{2 \times |13{,}200 - 0|}{2 \times 2 \times 13{,}200} = 0.5.
\end{equation*}
The history-aware policy reads $\Hist_t$ at 12:30: $\text{deficit}_B = 1$ and $\text{deficit}_A = 0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eqn:brkgafair}, or equivalently $w_B(s) = 3{,}474$ and $w_A(s) = 13{,}895$ COP/h in Eq.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_w} (projected to the end of the window), so the singleton goes to $B$, and likewise at 12:40 and 12:50. The end-of-day hourly earnings are $w_A = w_B = 6{,}600$ COP/h and $\FG = 0$, with the same coverage (six orders), the same connected time, and a difference in travel of a few hundred metres.

This example highlights two points. First, the per-window statistic cannot distinguish the two decisions: within each window after 12:00 the pay offered is $(2{,}200, 0)$ whichever courier is chosen, so a policy that is fair within the window, such as the \textbf{Hierarchical} benchmark, is indifferent between an end-of-day Gini coefficient of $0.5$ and one of $0$. Second, what distinguishes them is the component $\Hist_t$ of the state, which records that $A$ has already earned 6{,}600 COP during the same connected time in which $B$ has earned nothing. The effect compounds when couriers connect at different times, when routes dispatched in different windows differ in value, and when some couriers are systematically nearer to the stores, which is why the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy raises the end-of-day Gini coefficient on real days (Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}).

\subsection{The BRKGA decoder}
\label{sec:App_decoder}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder} is the decoder shared by \textbf{BRKGA}, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} (Section~\ref{sec:method}); the policies differ only in the score it minimises.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\textbf{Input:} keys $\kappa_o$ for ready orders, $\kappa_c$ for available couriers, candidate pairs $K_t$, score function $\text{score}(s, c)$\\
\textbf{Output:} assignment $A$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE{$A \gets \emptyset$; mark all couriers free}
\FOR{each store $r$ in decreasing order of $\max_{o: r_o = r} \kappa_o$}
  \FOR{each order $o$ of $r$ in decreasing order of $\kappa_o$, not yet assigned}
    \STATE{$\mathcal{S}_o \gets$ candidate routes headed by $o$ (singleton and the store bundle containing $o$)}
    \STATE{$\mathcal{C}_o \gets$ the $K^{\text{dec}}$ free couriers with highest $\kappa_c$ among $\{c : (c,s) \in K_t,\ s \in \mathcal{S}_o\}$}
    \IF{$\mathcal{C}_o \neq \emptyset$}
      \STATE{$(s^*, c^*) \gets \argmin \{\text{score}(s, c) : s \in \mathcal{S}_o,\ c \in \mathcal{C}_o,\ (c,s) \in K_t\}$}
      \STATE{$A \gets A \cup \{(c^*, s^*)\}$; mark $c^*$ busy and the orders of $s^*$ assigned}
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN{$A$}
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{BRKGA decoder shared by \textbf{BRKGA}, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}}
\label{alg:decoder}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Per-round formulation of MIP-Fair}
\label{sec:app_MIP}

At round $t$, let $C_t$ be the set of connected couriers, $C_t^{\text{free}} \subseteq C_t$ the free ones, $K_t$ the candidate pairs and $\mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}}$ the ready orders. Let $x_{cs} \in \{0,1\}$ for $(c,s) \in K_t$, $y_o \in \{0,1\}$ for $o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}}$, $w_c \ge 0$ for $c \in C_t$, $d_{ij} \ge 0$ for free--free pairs $i < j$, $\text{late}_o \ge 0$ and $v \ge 0$. The three stages of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_lex} are solved over the following constraints:

\begin{align}
\sum_{(c,s) \in K_t : o \in s} x_{cs} & \ge y_o, && \forall o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}} \label{eqn:app_cover} \\
\sum_{(c,s) \in K_t : o \in s} x_{cs} & \le 1, && \forall o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}} \label{eqn:app_once} \\
\sum_{s : (c,s) \in K_t} x_{cs} & \le 1, && \forall c \in C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_courier} \\
w_c & = w_c^0 \Bigl(1 - \sum_{s} x_{cs}\Bigr) + \sum_{s} x_{cs}\, w_c(s), && \forall c \in C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_w} \\
w_c & = w_c^0, && \forall c \in C_t \setminus C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_wfixed} \\
d_{ij} & \ge w_i - w_j, \quad d_{ij} \ge w_j - w_i, && \forall i < j \in C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_d}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
& \sum_{i<j \in C_t^{\text{free}}} d_{ij} + \sum_{i \in C_t^{\text{free}},\, j \notin C_t^{\text{free}}} \Bigl[\Bigl(1 - \sum_s x_{is}\Bigr)|w_i^0 - w_j^0| + \sum_s x_{is}\, |w_i(s) - w_j^0|\Bigr] \\
& \qquad + \sum_{i<j,\; i,j \notin C_t^{\text{free}}} |w_i^0 - w_j^0| - \alpha\, n \sum_{c \in C_t} w_c \;\le\; n\, W_{\text{ref}}\, v
\end{split}
\label{eqn:app_gini}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
\text{late}_o & \ge \sum_{(c,s) \in K_t : o \in s} x_{cs}\, \max\bigl(0, \hat\tau_{\text{drop}}(c, s, o) - d_o\bigr), && \forall o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}} \label{eqn:app_late} \\
& x_{cs} \in \{0,1\},\; y_o \in \{0,1\},\; d_{ij}, w_c, \text{late}_o, v \ge 0. && \label{eqn:app_dom}
\end{align}

Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_cover} links coverage to dispatched routes and Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:app_once}--\eqref{eqn:app_courier} ensure that each order is in at most one dispatched route and each courier receives at most one. Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:app_w}--\eqref{eqn:app_wfixed} give the projected hourly earnings, linear in $x$ for free couriers and fixed for the others. Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_d} defines the pairwise differences for free--free pairs only, and Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_gini} is the Gini constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_gini} with the free--fixed block written out as linear terms and the fixed--fixed block as a constant; $n = |C_t|$ and $W_{\text{ref}}$ is the mean of $w_c^0$ over $C_t$ (or $w^*$ when that mean is zero). Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_late} computes the projected lateness of each order under its dispatched route, and Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_dom} defines the domains. Stage 1 maximises $\sum_o y_o$; stage 2 keeps $\sum_o y_o \ge (1 - \varepsilon) \sum_o y_o^*$, with $y^*$ the stage-1 optimum, and minimises $v$; stage 3 fixes $v^*$ and minimises $\lambda_{\text{tt}} \sum_{(c,s)} \tau_s x_{cs} + \mu \sum_o \text{late}_o$. We use $\lambda_{\text{tt}} = 0.01$ per minute of travel and $\mu = 0.02$ per minute of lateness, the tie-breaking weights of the \textbf{ILP} and \textbf{Hierarchical} policies.

\paragraph{Size of the programme and order of the stages} This paragraph details Section~\ref{sec:mipfair}. Building $d_{ij}$ for all pairs is $O(n^2)$ per round, which is prohibitive with thousands of connected couriers: with 188 connected couriers the all-pairs model did not complete a two-hour slice of a Pereira day within four minutes of wall-clock time, and Bogot\'a would require 3.5 million pairs per round. The free--free block keeps $O(|C_t^{\text{free}}|^2)$ variables and the free--fixed block $O(|K_t|\,|C_t \setminus C_t^{\text{free}}|)$ coefficients; in the zones of Section~\ref{sec:instances} a round solves in $0.2$--$0.4$ s on average and in at most $7.6$ s (\ref{sec:App_metrics}), within the 10-second limit per stage. Coverage is placed first because the alternative order (equity first) collapses from a cold start: when every courier has earned nothing, any assignment raises the projected Gini coefficient, so \enquote{assign nothing} minimises $v$ in every round and the system never starts (coverage $0.000$ on the pilot day from a cold start; $0.310$ over the 27 runs of the ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}).

\subsection{Tuning of the policy parameters}
\label{sec:app_tuning}

The candidate prefilter of \textbf{MIP-Fair} (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}) keeps $k = 8$ couriers per route for budget reasons: with $k = 25$ a two-hour window of Bogot\'a did not finish within seven minutes, while with $k = 8$ it takes 4.5 minutes and covers 0.443 against 0.437 for \textbf{Greedy}. Table~\ref{tab:ablations} in \ref{sec:App_ablations} reports the two tuning ablations on the 24 instances: the BRKGA with population 20 and 20 generations leaves coverage unchanged and raises the end-of-day Gini coefficient by $0.007$ at almost three times the CPU time, and the \textbf{MIP-Fair} prefilter with $k = 25$ lowers it by $0.039$ and raises coverage by $0.008$ at eight times the CPU time (31 minutes per day on average, 227 at most, and beyond the four-hour timeout on the largest Bogot\'a day on four or five of the seeds, depending on the configuration); the deficit-aware prefilter of Section~\ref{sec:results_quality} obtains more than twice that reduction for the same order of CPU time. The settings of Section~\ref{sec:implementation} are therefore kept. In the pilot, the BRKGA-type policies take 9--10 seconds for a full day of Pereira and 30--35 seconds for a two-hour window of Bogot\'a, the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy 16 and 39 seconds, and \textbf{MIP-Fair} with $k = 8$ 36 seconds and 4.5 minutes, respectively; a BRKGA with population 20 and 20 generations did not change coverage on Pereira.

\subsection{Verification against enumeration and the all-pairs model}
\label{sec:App_verification}

The four pieces (BRKGA port, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, \textbf{MIP-Fair}, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}) are covered by unit tests against exhaustive enumeration on a test instance of three couriers and four orders: the candidate sets coincide with those of the \textbf{ILP} and the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy (18 pairs); the $\text{score}_{\text{fpo}}$ decoder reaches the exhaustive optimum of its fitness; \textbf{MIP-Fair} with $\alpha = 0$ coincides with the exhaustive lexicographic optimum under both priorities and both earnings bases, and with $\alpha = 1$ covers 4 of 4 orders like the \textbf{ILP}; the block linearisation reproduces the all-pairs model; and for \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}, with $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ alone all offers fall below the tariff, with $\lambda_{\text{def}}$ alone the under-paid courier receives an offer above the tariff and leaves the deficit, and expected coverage equals coverage times the acceptance rate under a fixed-probability model. On real rounds, both formulations of \textbf{MIP-Fair} ($\alpha = 0.30$, $k = 8$, first generation, manual regime, seed 1) were solved on the same candidate pairs and histories in every round of 11:00--13:00 on instance 10 and 12:00--14:00 on instance 4, the block decision driving the simulation: in all 117 rounds the Gini constraint was binding ($v^* > 0$) and the coverage and $v$ optima coincide exactly; the population had a median of 122 and 168 couriers, the pair variables fell from 7{,}381 and 14{,}028 to 78 and 105, and the mean solve time from 1.2 and 2.1 s to 0.04 and 0.05 s per round (\texttt{simulator/diag\_blocks.py}).

\subsection{Instances, parameters, factors and statistical procedure}
\label{sec:App_instances}

Table~\ref{tab:instances} lists the 24 instances described in Section~\ref{sec:instances}, Table~\ref{tab:parameters} the parameters of the simulation with their values, sources and evidence grade, and Table~\ref{tab:factors} the factors and levels of the computational study of Section~\ref{sec:design}; Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows a 3-km zone and Figure~\ref{fig:acceptance} the acceptance curve of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance}. Each day is projected on a plane centred on the median store location, discarding a few points hundreds of kilometres away.

\paragraph{Computational effort} Section~\ref{sec:design} gives the runs of each experiment. In total 15{,}175 runs and 1{,}317 CPU-hours: a run took 2.9 minutes on average in X1, 7.1 in the second generation of X3, 7.1 in X2, 18.7 in the ablations and 1.8 in the review experiment.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistical procedure} This paragraph details Section~\ref{sec:metrics}. For every run we record the two components of the cost of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:cost} separately, coverage (fraction of orders delivered) and mean and total lateness, the number of late orders, click-to-door mean and 90th percentile, the end-of-day $\FG$, $\FM$, $\FO$ and $\FD$, total payment, the number of active couriers, the mean bundle size, the mean and maximum solve time per round, and the wall-clock time; emissions follow from the per-courier distances by vehicle. The reported Gini coefficient is one and the same for all policies, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini} over the eligible couriers, computed ex post from the per-courier table of every run; the active-courier statistic $\FGa$ is reported beside it, and the scope used inside a policy affects what it optimises, not what is measured. To compare the policies, we compute for each policy $\pi$ and instance $i$ the average $\bar y_i^{\pi}$ of a metric over the ten seeds and report the mean over the 24 instances together with its standard error, $\mathrm{SE} = \mathrm{SD}/\sqrt{N}$ with $N = 24$ and SD the sample standard deviation across instances. The cost of the guarantee is the paired difference $\bar y_i^{\pi} - \bar y_i^{\textbf{Greedy}}$ with a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval (2{,}000 resamples) over the 24 instances (instance as block, seeds averaged within); the paired differences between a variant and its parent in Section~\ref{sec:results_quality} and in \ref{sec:App_gen2} and \ref{sec:App_ablations} are over the (instance, seed) pairs with the normal approximation $\pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$. The share of the gap to \textbf{Greedy} closed by a variant is $1 - (\bar F^{\text{var}} - \bar F^{\textbf{Greedy}})/(\bar F^{\text{par}} - \bar F^{\textbf{Greedy}})$, a ratio of means over instances on the same seeds, so that it can be recomputed from the tables. Compliance is the fraction of (instance, seed) pairs, which we call days for short, with $\FG \le \alpha$ by policy and $\alpha$, computed ex post, so the grid of $\alpha$ can be extended without new runs. Under the flat tariff $\FO$ coincides with $\FGa$ for every policy that pays the tariff, so it is not reported separately; $\FM$, click-to-door and the solve time per round are in \ref{sec:App_metrics}. The regime loss is the paired difference manual minus auto-accept. Frontiers are the mean over (instance, seed) of $(\FG, \text{coverage})$ and $(\FG, \text{lateness})$ at each level of the swept parameter, with the non-dominated envelope and the knee selected by the maximum distance from the chord between the extremes, applied identically to the three policies.

\subsection{Results by city}
\label{sec:App_by_city}

Table~\ref{tab:city} repeats the manual-regime block of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}, without \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, for each of the six cities, with the compliance with $\alpha = 0.40$ as the last column. The central result holds city by city: \textbf{Greedy} is between $0.275$ (Medell\'in) and $0.323$ (Pereira) on the end-of-day Gini coefficient over the eligible couriers and meets $\alpha = 0.40$ on 85--100\% of the days in every city (Pereira lowest), while the six order-centric policies are between $0.448$ (\textbf{ILP}, Bogot\'a and Medell\'in) and $0.643$ (\textbf{BRKGA}, Bucaramanga) and meet it on none. The guarantee is therefore uniformly within reach of the least-served-first rule, and the question of where it is cheap or expensive becomes the question of where the order-centric policies fall furthest from it. They fall furthest in the two smallest fleets, Bucaramanga and Pereira ($0.56$--$0.64$, with only 59--73\% of the eligible couriers active), and closest in Bogot\'a and Medell\'in ($0.45$--$0.51$, 79--87\% active), where couriers outnumber the work less than in Bucaramanga and Pereira (0.39--0.42 against 0.22--0.27 orders per connected courier-hour in the zones, pooled over the days of each city, \texttt{simulator/claims\_text.py}) and the best-placed courier changes more often. The coverage that \textbf{Greedy} gives up with respect to the best policy of the city is between $0.8$ points (Bogot\'a) and $1.5$ points (Cali and Bucaramanga), and its extra lateness against \textbf{Hierarchical}, the least late policy in every city, between $1.4$ (Bucaramanga) and $2.3$ minutes (Bogot\'a); the pay lever of \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} cuts the bill most where the fleet is small and routes are long (Pereira, 19\%; Bucaramanga, 16\%) and least in Bogot\'a and Medell\'in (7--9\%). The second-generation variants with $k_d = 25$ (five seeds) keep the ordering: under the manual regime they reach $0.37$--$0.38$ in Barranquilla, Bogot\'a and Medell\'in, $0.45$--$0.46$ in Cali and $0.50$--$0.55$ in Bucaramanga and Pereira, so the gap to \textbf{Greedy} that remains is again largest in the two smallest fleets.

\subsection{The second generation against its parents}
\label{sec:App_gen2}

Table~\ref{tab:gen2} reports the means of the second-generation configurations of X3 against their first-generation parents and \textbf{Greedy} on the same five seeds, and Table~\ref{tab:gen2_paired} reports, for every configuration, the paired difference with its first-generation parent on the same (instance, seed) pairs, the share of the gap to \textbf{Greedy} closed (ratio of means, Section~\ref{sec:metrics}) and the compliance with $\alpha = 0.40$ and $0.45$. The exposure basis alone moves $\FG$ by $0.004$--$0.025$; the prefilter moves it by $0.054$--$0.131$ for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} and by $0.021$--$0.027$ for \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}, with every interval excluding zero and coverage within $0.006$ of the parent.

\paragraph{Coverage slack, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}, CPU time and $k_d = 50$} This paragraph completes the discussion of the second generation in Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}. The coverage slack $\varepsilon$ of \textbf{MIP-Fair} does nothing ($\varepsilon = 0.05$ against $0$: $\FG$ within $0.002$ and coverage within $0.001$), which confirms that what bound the Gini block was the candidate set and not the lexicographic priority. \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} responds least to the prefilter (9--11\% of the gap over the two regimes) because its fitness has no equity term: only the prefilter reads the deficit, and the decoder then scores on coverage alone. The price of $k_d$ is CPU time: \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with $k_d = 25$ costs $4.3$--$4.9$ minutes per day on average and $25$ at most, twice its parent; \textbf{MIP-Fair} with $k_d = 25$ costs $11$--$24$ minutes on average and up to $110$ on a Bogot\'a day under the manual regime, five times its parent, for the same end-of-day Gini, so the heuristic is the practical choice at this scale. Half of the gap to \textbf{Greedy} remains, and the ablation with $k_d = 50$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablations} in \ref{sec:App_ablations}) shows that for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} it is a matter of candidates, since $\FG$ keeps falling to $0.404$ under the manual regime and $0.353$ under auto-accept at unchanged coverage and $1.3$ times the CPU time, whereas for \textbf{MIP-Fair} the curve flattens ($0.412$ and $0.399$) while the CPU time grows to 51 minutes per day: for the exact round model the one-route-per-courier structure and the solver limit bind before the candidate set does.

\subsection{Compliance and earnings by population}
\label{sec:App_population}

Table~\ref{tab:compliance} reports the compliance with the end-of-day guarantee by policy and regime, Table~\ref{tab:scope} reports the end-of-day Gini coefficient by population discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}, Table~\ref{tab:eligibility} the same statistic with eligibility thresholds of 0.5, 1 and 2 hours, and Figure~\ref{fig:violin} the distribution of end-of-day hourly earnings by policy discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results_decisions}.

\paragraph{The inversion of the active-courier statistic} This paragraph details Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}. Table~\ref{tab:scope} shows how the concentration of work inverts the statistic that the fairness literature, and the first version of this study, computed over the active couriers: $\FGa$ is lowest for \textbf{ILP} ($0.411$), then \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} ($0.456$, $0.459$), and highest for \textbf{Greedy} ($0.514$) and \textbf{Hierarchical} ($0.530$); in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} it is lowest of all for \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} ($0.339$), the least equitable policy on Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}. Two manoeuvres produce the inversion. Idle couriers leave the active population, so concentrating work on fewer couriers lowers $\FGa$ while raising $\FG$; and the 7\% of couriers who connect for less than one hour, to whom \textbf{Greedy} dispatches because they are free and nearby, enter $\FGa$ with hourly earnings of up to $110{,}000$ COP (one delivery of 2{,}200 COP within a few minutes of connecting) and raise it, whereas Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini} excludes them. Restricted to the eligible couriers that were active, \textbf{Greedy} is still the most equitable ($0.28$ against $0.34$--$0.39$), so the first manoeuvre is the larger one.

\paragraph{Who receives which route, and what it pays} This paragraph details Section~\ref{sec:results_decisions}. Figure~\ref{fig:violin} compares the distribution of end-of-day hourly earnings over the eligible couriers under \textbf{Greedy} and under each order-centric policy at its best configuration, pooled over the 24 instances of one seed (23{,}947 couriers, manual regime); the x-axis lists the policies, the y-axis the earnings per connected hour, and the share of couriers who end the day idle is printed under each violin. The statutory minimum $w^* = 4{,}368$ COP/h lies above the 99th percentile of every distribution, which is the content of $\FD \ge 0.97$. The policies do not compress the distribution of \textbf{Greedy}; they stretch it at both ends. In this pooled one-seed sample, \textbf{Greedy} leaves 3\% of the eligible couriers idle and its interquartile range is $523$--$877$ COP/h around a median of $688$; the six order-centric policies leave 19--25\% idle, their first quartile is between $0$ (\textbf{BRKGA}) and $209$ COP/h, their median is similar ($548$--$621$) and their third quartile is higher ($1{,}067$--$1{,}209$ COP/h): the orders that \textbf{Greedy} spreads over the couriers who happen to be free go, under an order-centric policy, to the couriers who are best placed, who are then best placed again. The reduction of the Gini coefficient of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini} under \textbf{Greedy} therefore comes from below (fewer idle couriers) and from above (fewer high earners) at once, at the same total pay: the mean is $803$ COP/h under \textbf{Greedy} and $708$--$797$ under the policies that pay the tariff. \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} shifts the whole distribution down (mean $690$, median $548$) because it pays 12\% less, and still leaves 23\% idle. No courier crosses $w^*$ under \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} either. Bundling plays no role in these differences: the mean bundle size is $1.03$ orders per route under \textbf{Greedy} and the BRKGA-type policies, $1.01$ under \textbf{MIP-Fair} and $1.00$ under the \textbf{ILP} and \textbf{Hierarchical} policies. For \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} the pay goes where acceptance is at stake. The total payment is 88\% of the tariff under the manual regime and 80\% under auto-accept (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), while the median pay per kilometre received by an active courier is $929$ COP/km against $816$ under \textbf{Greedy} in the manual regime ($789$ against $669$ under auto-accept): the multiplier raises the pay of the long routes that would otherwise be rejected and lowers that of the short ones that would not, so the bill falls while the pay per kilometre of what is accepted stays above the threshold. In the auto-accept regime, where nothing is rejected, the same rule only lowers the bill and leaves $\FD$ unchanged.

\paragraph{Intraday trajectories} This paragraph details the reading of Figure~\ref{fig:intraday} in Section~\ref{sec:results_history} (one Bogot\'a day, instance 0, manual regime, seed 1). The x-axis is the time of day, the y-axis the Gini coefficient, recomputed every ten minutes from the delivery log; the horizontal line marks $\alpha = 0.40$, the upper bound of the inequality band. The four trajectories start near $1$: at 08:00, 525 eligible couriers are connected and about forty have delivered, so almost everyone is at zero, and the coefficient falls as orders arrive and the fleet is put to work. The policies separate between 10:00 and 16:00, the hours in which most of the fleet connects. \textbf{Greedy} puts the new couriers to work as they arrive and its trajectory falls from $0.79$ at 10:00 to $0.41$ at 14:00, crosses $\alpha = 0.40$ at about 14:10 and, after a bump to $0.28$ around 16:40, ends the day at $0.23$. \textbf{Hierarchical} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} keep assigning to the couriers already in motion, so their trajectories fall more slowly ($0.59$ and $0.57$ at 14:00) and never reach $\alpha$: they end the day at $0.48$ and $0.47$ with 15--16\% of the eligible couriers idle. The right panel shows why neither policy reacted: on the active couriers both stay at $0.32$--$0.39$ between 12:00 and 16:00, below $\alpha$ until 16:30 (\textbf{Hierarchical}) and 19:50 (\textbf{MIP-Fair}), so the statistic they read reports compliance during the hours in which the fleet grows, while the end-of-day statistic of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} ends $0.07$--$0.08$ above $\alpha$, because the couriers who make the difference are not in their population. The second-generation \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, which admits the idle eligible couriers with the largest deficit as candidates in every round, follows the order-centric trajectory until noon and then bends towards \textbf{Greedy}: $0.51$ at 14:00, below $\alpha$ at 15:50, and $0.36$ at the end of the day with 97\% of the eligible couriers active and coverage $0.780$, the highest of the four.

\paragraph{BRKGA decoder and size} Scoring a chromosome by the finishing time instead of the finishing time per order lowers $\FG$ by $0.007$ and coverage by $0.001$ at $1.4$ times the CPU time. A population of 20 with 20 generations leaves coverage unchanged, raises $\FG$ by $0.007$ (worse in 75 of the 120 pairs) and costs almost three times the CPU time: the round objective is a coverage objective, and a longer search improves a quantity that the orders whose deadline can no longer be met already saturate while doing nothing for the end-of-day statistic. The $10 \times 10$ setting and the per-order score are retained.

\paragraph{Scope and basis of the \textbf{MIP-Fair} constraint, and prefilter $k$} Three ablations tighten the round constraint of \textbf{MIP-Fair}: the Gini over the free couriers only, the total-earnings basis and the prefilter with $k = 25$. The three lower $\FG$ by $0.032$--$0.049$ and raise coverage by $0.006$--$0.008$, with 77--98 of the pairs better than the base. The expectation that the free-only scope would raise the end-of-day Gini because the free couriers are a biased subset is not borne out: $\FG$ falls by $0.049$ and the active-courier Gini from $0.460$ to $0.413$ on the same 115 pairs. None of the three reaches the second-generation variant with $k_d = 25$ ($\FG = 0.435$, Table~\ref{tab:gen2}), and all three cost eight to nine times the CPU time of the base (31--33 minutes per day on average and up to four hours on a Bogot\'a day), which is consistent with rounds that run to the 10-second solver limit once the constraint binds. A tighter round constraint is therefore not a substitute for the candidate set. The total-earnings basis in \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} moves $\FG$ by $0.005$: the weighted score reads the deficit only through the $K^{\text{dec}} = 3$ candidates of the decoder, so the basis matters little, as the exposure basis of the second generation also showed.

\paragraph{Equity-first priority} The lexicographic order with equity before coverage, the first design of \textbf{MIP-Fair}, reproduces the collapse of the pilot on the 27 runs completed: coverage $0.310$ against $0.753$ for the base, a Gini of $0.195$ obtained by not assigning (47\% of the eligible couriers active, lateness nine minutes lower because few orders are served) and 36 times the CPU time. The paper's \textbf{MIP-Fair} keeps coverage first for this reason.

\paragraph{Coverage slack} The slack $\varepsilon \in \{0.02, 0.05\}$ leaves $\FG$ and coverage within $0.001$ of the base, with 25--28 pairs worse and 25--37 better out of 120; this is the first-generation counterpart of the $\varepsilon$ result in Table~\ref{tab:gen2_paired}, and confirms that the lexicographic priority was not what bound the Gini block.

\paragraph{$k_d = 50$} The last two rows of each block answer the question left open in Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}: whether the half of the gap to \textbf{Greedy} that remains at $k_d = 25$ is a matter of candidates or of the one-route-per-courier structure of the round. For \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} it is a matter of candidates: $\FG$ falls from $0.436$ to $0.404$ under the manual regime and from $0.415$ to $0.353$ under auto-accept, no pair is worse than at $k_d = 25$, coverage is unchanged, 54\% and 73\% of the days meet $\alpha = 0.40$, and the CPU time grows by a third (6 minutes per day on average, 34 at most). The $k_d$ curve of the heuristic, $0.533 \to 0.436 \to 0.404$ (manual) and $0.535 \to 0.415 \to 0.353$ (auto-accept) for $k_d \in \{0, 25, 50\}$, the $k_d = 0$ point being the activity-basis parent, is still descending. For \textbf{MIP-Fair} the curve flattens: $0.542 \to 0.465 \to 0.435 \to 0.412$ (manual) and $0.532 \to 0.426 \to 0.401 \to 0.399$ (auto-accept) for $k_d \in \{0, 8, 25, 50\}$, the last step within noise under auto-accept and with a coverage loss of $0.004$ under the manual regime, while the CPU time grows to 51 minutes per day on average and almost four hours on the largest Bogot\'a day. For the exact round model the structure of the round and the solver limit bind before the candidate set does.

\subsection{Supply inside the bonus ladder and populations not based on hours}
\label{sec:App_objections}

This subsection reports two checks of the central result computed from the instances and the per-courier tables of the runs, without new runs (\texttt{analysis\_objections.py}; seeds 1--5, shared by X1, the second generation and the $k_d = 50$ supplement).

\paragraph{Supply} Over the 24 zones the recorded fleet gives $0.40$ orders per connected courier-hour. By clock hour, the busiest hour of each zone (12:00--14:00 in 15 instances, 19:00--20:00 in nine) reaches a median of $0.70$ ($0.30$--$1.26$); $9.0\%$ of the connected courier-hours fall in clock hours with at least $0.75$ orders per courier-hour and $0.9\%$, in two instances, in clock hours with at least the $1.15$ that surveyed couriers report inside the bonus ladder. With the fleet thinned to one courier in three the share at $1.15$ or more is $43\%$ ($0$--$64\%$ by instance, median $39\%$). The survey implies about a quarter of the time inside the ladder, so the full fleet understates and the thinned fleet overstates in-ladder conditions, and the two scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:results_sensitivity} bracket them. The clock-hour reading is generous to the ladder, since the survey ratio is a daily average. Realised per courier, at most $3.3\%$ of the eligible couriers reach $1.15$ orders per connected hour over the day under any policy ($1.7$--$2.0\%$ under \textbf{Greedy}), with at most $1\%$ of their connected hours.

\paragraph{Populations not based on hours} Table~\ref{tab:alt_population} recomputes the end-of-day Gini coefficient, idle couriers at zero, over populations that do not use the one-hour threshold: the couriers connected at any moment of the zone's busiest clock hour; the couriers connected during either of its two busiest hours, with earnings divided by the orders placed in the zone while each was connected (a demand-weighted exposure that uses no hours); the eligible couriers with the same exposure; and all the couriers of the zone, with either normalisation. On the busiest-hour populations and on the demand-weighted exposure over the eligible couriers, \textbf{Greedy} is the most equitable policy in both regimes, and it is below the best first-generation order-centric policy on 24 of the 24 instances under manual acceptance and on 21--23 under auto-accept. Without any floor on connection time the ranking narrows, and under auto-accept \textbf{ILP} ends below \textbf{Greedy}: \textbf{Greedy} is above the best first-generation policy on 9 (manual) and 15 (auto-accept) instances in hourly earnings and on 13 and 17 in demand-weighted exposure, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}: it dispatches to couriers connected for a few minutes, who enter with hourly earnings far above the rest. \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with $k_d = 50$ is the most equitable policy over all the couriers.

\paragraph{Benchmark and candidate set} \textbf{Greedy} ranks the free couriers by the orders delivered so far (\texttt{policies.py}), which under the flat tariff is proportional to daily earnings, not to earnings per connected hour, and it does not know which couriers will be connected long enough to be eligible; it is therefore not tuned to the statistic of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}. The deficit-aware prefilter of the second generation already applies its least-served-first rule to the candidate set; a decoder that also breaks coverage ties least served first is the natural hybrid, which we leave for future work. Whether a per-round model over all the free couriers would close the rest of the gap is open: with $k_d = 50$ \textbf{MIP-Fair} flattens at $0.40$--$0.41$ while its CPU time grows to 51 minutes per day, so a Bogot\'a zone calls for column generation over courier--route pairs rather than a larger prefilter.
