\title{Who gets the work? End-of-day earnings fairness and price-responsive couriers in meal-delivery dispatching}

\author[1]{Daniel Giraldo-Herrera\,\orcidlink{0009-0003-8243-723X}\corref{cor1}} \ead{ds.giraldoh@uniandes.edu.co} \cortext[cor1]{Corresponding Author}
\author[1]{David \'Alvarez-Mart\'inez} \ead{d.alvarezm@uniandes.edu.co}

\affiliation[1]{organization={Department of Industrial Engineering, Universidad de los Andes}, addressline={Cra. 1 No 18a-12}, postcode={111711}, city={Bogot\'a}, country={Colombia}}

\begin{abstract}
We study dispatching in which a platform repeatedly offers work and pay to self-employed agents who may reject it, and is accountable for how their earnings are distributed at the end of the day rather than within each decision window. In meal delivery, fairness-aware dispatching has so far assumed perfect acceptance and no equity history. We formulate it as a constrained Markov decision process with an equity history per courier, an end-of-day Gini constraint on hourly earnings over the couriers connected for at least one hour, idle ones included, and logit acceptance on pay per kilometre under manual and auto-accept regimes. We develop three cost function approximations that read the history every round, including a per-window mixed-integer programme with an exact block linearisation of the Gini constraint, and compare them with five benchmarks on 24 operating days from six Colombian cities. The central finding is a question of population: a courier-centric dispatcher that serves the least-served courier first ends the day at a Gini coefficient of 0.30, whereas every order-centric policy ends between 0.52 and 0.56 under manual acceptance because it leaves a quarter of the eligible couriers idle. Reading exposure and offering the most-deprived couriers to the solver closes about half of that gap at no coverage cost. With a third of the fleet, a supply sensitivity scenario, the three policies tested end between 0.21 and 0.25.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Dispatching problems arise when a limited set of agents must be matched repeatedly to requests that appear over time. In a corporate fleet, the agents are vehicles: an assignment is executed as soon as it is made, and the quality of a dispatching policy is measured by the service it delivers to customers \citep{Dantzig1959,pillac2013review}. In platform operations, however, the agents are self-employed workers who decide whether to execute each assignment and who earn their living from the sum of the assignments they execute over a day. Two features of such settings are absent from the corporate model. First, an assignment is an offer: whether it is accepted depends on what it pays relative to the effort it asks for, so the transition of the system is endogenous to the pay decision. Second, the platform is accountable for a distributional outcome, the earnings of its workers at the end of the day, which no single decision window determines but every decision window affects.

Meal delivery is the setting in which these features are sharpest. On-demand platforms coordinate restaurants, customers and a crowd of couriers through an assignment decision repeated every few minutes. The operational literature on the meal delivery routing problem (MDRP) has made that decision efficient: rolling-horizon re-optimisation, order bundling and anticipatory assignment deliver high coverage with short click-to-door (placement to delivery) times \citep{reyes2018meal,yildiz2019provably,Ulmer2021}. The couriers' side has received less attention: a courier earns over a day, not over a two-minute window, so a dispatcher that balances work within each window can still end the day with very unequal hourly earnings when couriers connect at different times or some systematically receive the better routes. Moreover, a courier is not a vehicle: in the field, couriers reason in pay per kilometre and reject long, low-paying routes, and, in the platform we study, the workforce has moved to an auto-accept regime, backed by sanctions, because manual rejection erodes coverage (Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description}). Similar structures arise in ride-hailing \citep{lesmana2019balancing}, in crowdsourced last-mile delivery \citep{sobhanan2026equity} and in gig platforms that guarantee an hourly wage \citep{nair2022work4food}: a platform decides who receives which work and at what pay, workers respond to that pay, and the fairness outcome is defined over a horizon longer than the decision window.

Methodologically, these problems are challenging because the fairness guarantee, the acceptance behaviour and the routing decision are coupled within the same sequential process: the value of offering a route depends on whether it will be accepted, which depends on the pay, and the earnings accumulated by every courier determine whether the end-of-day guarantee can still be met. Anticipation is therefore required, not of future demand, which rolling-horizon dispatching handles procedurally, but of the distributional consequence of the current assignment at the end of the day: who receives each route and what the offer pays must be decided window by window while keeping track of what every courier has earned and worked so far.

We address this problem as a constrained Markov decision process (CMDP, \citealt{altman1999constrained}). The state carries, in addition to the positions of couriers and the pool of orders, an \emph{equity history} $\Hist_t$ with the earnings, orders and busy time accumulated by every courier. The constraint bounds by a tolerated inequality $\alpha$ the end-of-day Gini coefficient of hourly earnings over the \emph{eligible} couriers, those connected for at least one hour, with the idle ones counted at zero. The transition draws the acceptance of every offer from a logit on pay per kilometre, estimated from field data, with a manual regime in which couriers reject and an auto-accept regime in which they do not. We then develop three policies that read $\Hist_t$ when deciding the current window: \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} biases the decoder of a biased random-key genetic algorithm by each courier's equity deficit, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} adds a pay gene per courier that sets the offer within a bounded band around the tariff, and \textbf{MIP-Fair} solves a per-window mixed-integer programme (MIP) in which the Gini constraint is written on the projected end-of-window history and linearised exactly by blocks of courier pairs. In terms of the framework of \cite{powell2021reinforcement}, all three are cost function approximations: they modify the problem solved in each window with terms computed from $\Hist_t$ instead of learning a value of the future.

Three research questions guide the study. First, does an end-of-day fairness guarantee require the equity history in the state, or can fairness enforced within each window deliver it? Second, which lever satisfies the guarantee at the lowest cost in coverage and punctuality, choosing who receives each route or choosing what the offer pays, and does the answer depend on the acceptance regime? Third, under which operational conditions, acceptance regime, logit threshold, tolerated inequality and fleet composition, is the guarantee cheap or expensive, and which $\alpha$ is sustainable for a platform? Using 24 operating days from six Colombian cities with acceptance calibrated in the field (135 surveys, 44 interviews), we compare the three policies against two courier-centric greedy dispatchers, \textbf{Greedy}, which serves the least-served courier first, and \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, which serves the couriers in order of log-in, a coverage integer linear programme (ILP), a coverage-only BRKGA and a hierarchical policy that is fair within each window. The computational study establishes the following insights:

\begin{compactitem}
    \item The guarantee is a property of the day, not of the window: a policy that is fair within every window (\textbf{Hierarchical}) ends the day more unequal than \textbf{Greedy} ($0.53$ against $0.30$); among the order-centric policies, only those that read exposure and offer the most-deprived couriers to the solver halve that gap. \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, the same dispatcher with the couriers in order of log-in, ends at $0.75$, the most unequal of all policies, so the equity of \textbf{Greedy} comes from its least-served-first priority.
    \item The size of the gap depends on supply: in a sensitivity scenario with the recorded fleet thinned to one courier in three, \textbf{Greedy} and two \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} variants keep 96--100\% of the eligible couriers active and end between $0.21$ and $0.25$, and the gap of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} to \textbf{Greedy} falls from $0.23$--$0.24$ to $0.03$--$0.04$.
    \item Coverage is identical across policies under auto-accept and within $1.2$ points of \textbf{Greedy} under manual acceptance for every policy but the coverage ILP, so the cost of the guarantee is paid in \emph{who} receives each route and in lateness ($0.5$--$1.8$ minutes under manual acceptance and $0.2$--$1.1$ under auto-accept against the six order-centric policies).
    \item The acceptance regime matters more than the policy for coverage: the regime loss is $1.6$--$4.9$ points against $1.2$ between \textbf{Greedy} and the best order-centric policy ($3.3$ including the coverage ILP).
    \item The pay lever cuts the bill rather than inequality: \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} pays 12\% (manual) to 20\% (auto-accept) less than the tariff at the same coverage under auto-accept and $1.6$ points less than \textbf{BRKGA} under manual acceptance, a transfer from couriers who already earn below the minimum wage to the platform.
    \item Dispatching alone cannot lift couriers to the statutory minimum wage at the current tariff (97--100\% remain below it under every policy, 92\% in the survey); what it can do is distribute the shortfall evenly.
    \item The three criteria for $\alpha$ give the inequality band $[0.30, 0.40]$ rather than a point: $0.30$ is the courier-centric anchor, the end-of-day Gini coefficient of \textbf{Greedy}, which no order-centric policy of the main experiment reaches on any day, and $0.40$ the operating point $\alpha^*$, which the second-generation \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with the widest candidate set ($k_d = 50$) meets on 54--73\% of the days; choosing between them is a managerial trade-off of a point of coverage and about a minute of lateness against five to ten points of Gini.
\end{compactitem}

The paper makes three contributions. First, as a modelling contribution, we formulate meal-delivery dispatching as a CMDP with an intertemporal equity history in the state, price-endogenous acceptance and an end-of-day Gini constraint over a population the dispatcher cannot shrink: every courier connected for at least one hour, idle ones at zero. A numerical example shows why per-window fairness does not imply end-of-day fairness. Second, as a diagnostic and methodological contribution, we show that the population and the basis of the history decide the outcome: computed over the active couriers, the statistic inverts the ranking of the policies, and a history read as earnings per busy hour cannot see the couriers who never receive work. We develop three cost function approximations for the CMDP, including a per-window programme whose Gini constraint is linearised exactly by blocks of courier pairs, verified against the all-pairs model on 117 real rounds, and we derive the remedy that the diagnosis implies: an exposure basis for the history and a deficit-aware prefilter that offers the idle couriers to the solver, which halves the gap to the least-served-first rule at no coverage cost. Third, as a managerial contribution, we measure the cost of the guarantee on 24 real operating days from six cities with field-calibrated acceptance: the equity of a courier-centric dispatcher comes from its least-served-first priority rather than from optimisation (in order of log-in the same dispatcher is the most unequal policy); the guarantee is paid mainly in who receives each route and in lateness rather than in coverage; the acceptance regime moves coverage more than the policy does; and in a supply sensitivity scenario with a third of the recorded fleet the gap between \textbf{Greedy} and \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} falls to $0.03$--$0.04$, so the recommendation is conditional on supply. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first MDRP formulation that combines an end-of-day inequality constraint over a population the dispatcher cannot shrink, an equity history in the state and courier acceptance that responds to the pay the platform decides.

\paragraph{Problem-related literature}

The MDRP was defined by \cite{reyes2018meal} as a rolling-horizon, dynamic-deterministic problem: at each re-optimisation epoch a deterministic instance is solved and the horizon rolls forward as new orders arrive, so uncertainty is handled procedurally rather than through anticipation. Their public instance library (Grubhub) has become the standard benchmark. \cite{yildiz2019provably} obtained provably high-quality solutions for a deterministic variant. \cite{steever2019dynamic} revise assignments whenever an order arrives or traffic changes; \cite{Ulmer2021} delay assignment until preparation-time uncertainty resolves and anticipate it with approximate dynamic programming; \cite{liu2021multiperiod} dispatch and route on-demand deliveries from stores with random demand. Later contributions address shared logistics \citep{bi2023meal}, courier satisfaction through dynamic operating regions \citep{auad2023courier}, order batching for crowdsourced fleets \citep{simoni2023crowdsourced} and ghost kitchens \citep{neria2024restaurant}. For the broader class of stochastic dynamic vehicle routing problems we refer to \cite{SOEFFKER2022801} and \cite{hildebrandt2022opportunities}, and for their modelling as sequential decision processes to \cite{ulmer2020modeling}. Across these models, acceptance is perfect or exogenous and couriers are treated as vehicles. This literature motivates the \textbf{Greedy} and \textbf{ILP} benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:Benchmark}: a courier-centric dispatcher in which each free courier takes its best route, and the exact per-window coverage dispatcher that \textbf{MIP-Fair} extends.

Fairness has entered delivery and ride-hailing platforms mostly as \emph{balance}. \cite{wen2010dynamic} balance workload and income opportunities within a multi-period routing model, and \cite{liao2020multi} balance workload in meal delivery with a two-stage evolutionary algorithm (NSGA-II), arguing that an equitable distribution is itself an incentive. \cite{lesmana2019balancing} define fairness as income inequality among ride-sourcing drivers and trace the efficiency--fairness frontier with a reassignment algorithm applied batch by batch. FairFoody \citep{gupta2022fairfoody} and WORK4FOOD \citep{nair2022work4food} treat fairness as income distribution among couriers in Indian food-delivery data: the former balances earnings by assignment, the latter guarantees a minimum hourly wage by controlling the number of active couriers and paying for idle time. \cite{martinez2024exploring} explore fairness constraints in food-delivery routing and scheduling on UK data and find moderate efficiency costs, and \cite{sobhanan2026equity} give bi-objective equity--cost frontiers for crowdsourced last-mile delivery. These works share two limits that this paper removes: fairness is enforced per window or per instance, or amortised over the day through an objective or a reward term rather than constrained at its end, and couriers accept whatever is assigned. The per-window logic motivates our \textbf{Hierarchical} benchmark, which balances the pay offered among the free couriers of each window and ignores the history (Section~\ref{sec:Benchmark}). On the economic side, \cite{reyes2018meal} and \cite{yildiz2019provably} include a shift-level guaranteed minimum set by the company, \cite{bajec2022strategic} use Shapley-value incentives for sustainable crowdshipping, and the algorithmic-fairness literature shows that short- and long-horizon fairness can diverge \citep{corbettdavies2023measure}. None of these integrates pay as a dispatching decision to which acceptance responds. The Gini constraint itself belongs to inequity-averse optimisation \citep{karsu2015inequity,ogryczak2000inequality}; in ride-hailing, \cite{suhr2019twosided} balance driver income per active time over repeated matchings under perfect acceptance, and \cite{xu2020trade} trade efficiency for a max--min match rate across drivers under exogenous acceptance probabilities.

Courier behaviour has been modelled mainly as availability. Reinforcement-learning approaches \citep{ahamed2020deep,bozanta2022courier} include courier availability in the state but keep acceptance exogenous, and \cite{haferkamp2023heatmap} address repositioning. The closest precedent to endogenous acceptance is the discrete-event framework of \cite{quintero2020computational}, in which the probability of accepting a notification depends on the assignment distance and a courier-level parameter, but not on the pay offered. Customer acceptance has been anticipated in delivery routing \citep{ULMER2020183}, but there it is the platform that accepts requests, not the agent who accepts work. No MDRP study models acceptance as a function of the offered pay, although a courier offered a long, low-paying route is more likely to reject it, which biases both coverage and fairness estimates. The empirical literature on gig work documents the two levers we model: pay shapes the supply of self-scheduling workers \citep{cachon2017surge,allon2023gig}, and crowdsourced delivery has been planned with occasional drivers whose participation and compensation are decisions \citep{archetti2016occasional,dayarian2020crowdshipping,ulmer2020workforce}. \citet{cheng2025realtime} train deep Q-networks for dispatching and idle-courier steering on a fixed fleet paid by the hour; the fairness of the workload is reported ex post as the standard deviation of orders and delivery time per courier, but it is neither optimised nor part of the state.

\paragraph{Methodological literature}

Our policies belong to the class of cost function approximations in the framework of \cite{powell2021reinforcement} and \cite{POWELL2019795}: the problem solved in each window is modified by terms, the equity deficit, the projected Gini excess and the pay excess, that encode the end-of-day consequence of the current decision, without learning a value function. Constrained MDPs are usually solved with Lagrangian or primal--dual methods \citep{altman1999constrained,borkar2005actor}; our constraint is on a terminal non-linear functional, outside that machinery (Section~\ref{sec:SDP}), which is why we use cost function approximations. This is the class of parametrised policies of the dynamic routing literature \citep{SOEFFKER2022801}, whose parameters are tuned by enumeration or learned by simulation \citep{stein2024learning}; we tune ours over a grid and report the full frontier, since the trade-off itself is the object of study. Two methodological components are specific to this paper. The first is the biased random-key genetic algorithm \citep{goncalves2011biased} with rolling horizon that we ported from our earlier work on the MDRP \citep{giraldo2024grasp,giraldo2026brkga}; the decoder is the component that reads $\Hist_t$. The second is the linearisation of the Gini constraint, which in its natural form requires one variable per pair of couriers; because a courier takes at most one route per window, only the pairs of free couriers need variables, and we verify the reformulation against the all-pairs model. The Gini coefficient is used in its mean-absolute-difference form \citep{yitzhaki1979relative}. To the best of our knowledge, the proposed policies are the first to be evaluated against an end-of-day inequality constraint on real meal-delivery operations with acceptance that responds to the pay the platform decides.

\section{Problem definition}
\label{sec:problem_definition}

In this section, we define the fair meal-delivery dispatching problem and formulate it as a constrained sequential decision process. We first describe the problem and the field evidence on courier behaviour that shapes it. We then define the components of the process. An illustrative numerical example connecting the formulation to the dispatching decisions, and showing why per-window fairness does not imply end-of-day fairness, is provided in \ref{sec:example} of the supplementary material.

\subsection{Problem description}
\label{sec:Problem_description}

A platform connects a set of stores $R$, a set of users $U$ and a set of couriers $C$ over one operating day. Stores are known in advance; couriers are revealed at log-in with their position, vehicle and declared shift (the on and off times of the data); orders are revealed at placement time. Every two minutes, the dispatcher assigns to each available courier at most one route, where a route collects up to $Q$ orders from a single store and delivers them to their users, and offers it with a pay. The courier accepts or rejects the offer. Accepted routes are executed (pick-up, drop-off); the orders of rejected routes return to the pool for the next window. Couriers are notified only while idle or picking up. An order is cancelled, and counted against coverage, when no feasible assignment exists before its hard service deadline. We assume that each user places at most one order per day and that a cancelled order does not re-enter the system, as in the public MDRP instances \citep{reyes2018meal}.

Three aspects distinguish this problem from the corporate MDRP, and each is supported by field evidence collected with couriers of the platform in Bogot\'a (44 interviews in 2020--2021 and an intercept survey of 135 couriers in May--June 2021; the full assumption-versus-evidence table is in the online supplement). First, \emph{acceptance responds to pay per kilometre}. Couriers reason in pay per kilometre (\enquote{nobody takes 4.1 km for 3{,}300}); in the survey, pay (importance 5.71 on a 7-point scale) and distance (5.65) are the dominant factors in that order. A logit on $\ln(\text{pay/km})$ fitted to 32 acceptance decisions by 11 interviewees gives an indifference threshold of 903 Colombian pesos (COP) per kilometre (95\% interval [615; 1{,}242]) and slope 2.89 ([2.02; 5.23]). Second, \emph{there are two decision regimes}. In the 2020 interviews no courier auto-accepted and rejection was common; by 2021 most interviewees and 64\% of surveyed couriers auto-accepted, behind which are platform sanctions for rejecting (60\% of surveyed couriers had been blocked). In the auto-accept regime the courier's decision is whether to be connected at all, which the data record as on and off times. Third, \emph{the tariff is flat in distance}. The per-kilometre rate estimated from the interviews is indistinguishable from zero (155 COP/km, 95\% interval [$-103$; 413]); the survey reports 2{,}200 COP per order outside the platform's bonus ladder. We therefore model a flat tariff and treat per-offer pay as a policy instrument, not as a description of current practice.

The evidence also fixes the fairness outcome. Median hourly earnings while connected were 2{,}622 COP in the survey and 3{,}957 COP in the interviews; 91.9\% of surveyed couriers earned less than the 2021 statutory minimum wage per hour (4{,}368 COP), and the platform guarantees no hourly pay. The Gini coefficient of daily income per connected hour among surveyed couriers (the income of a working day over the hours connected per working day) is 0.41 outside the bonus ladder and 0.28 inside it (0.39 and 0.26 for daily income). We therefore measure fairness on hourly earnings at the end of the day, normalised by connected time so that connecting late is not penalised, over the couriers connected for at least one hour, with the idle ones counted at zero so that the dispatcher cannot improve the statistic by leaving couriers without work, and we use the two values per connected hour as anchors for the tolerated inequality. Service and decision times are taken from the same sources: a median in-store wait of 20 minutes with a purchased order (60 minutes in the stressed interview accounts) and a median decision time of 16 seconds for an offer displayed for 20 seconds.

The goal of the platform is to find a dispatching and pay-setting policy that minimises expected undelivered orders and lateness over the day while guaranteeing that the end-of-day Gini coefficient of the eligible couriers' hourly earnings does not exceed a tolerated value $\alpha$.

\subsection{Sequential decision process}
\label{sec:SDP}

The problem is stochastic and dynamic: orders are revealed over the day, preparation times are uncertain, and the acceptance of every offer is a random variable whose distribution depends on the pay the platform decides. Because assignment and pay decisions are made every two minutes and change the earnings and availability of couriers for the rest of the day, we model the problem as a sequential decision process \citep{powell2021reinforcement,ulmer2020modeling,cuellar2023} with a constraint on a terminal quantity, a constrained Markov decision process \citep{altman1999constrained}. In the following, we define its components: decision point and state, decisions, exogenous information and transition, cost function, and the constrained objective.

\paragraph{Decision point and state}

The process consists of decision points $t \in T$ with $T = \{0, 1, \dots, t_{\max}\}$, one at the start of every two-minute window of the day; we refer to the dispatching decision made at $t$ as a \emph{round}. At decision point $t$, the state $S_t$ contains the information accessible to the dispatcher: the couriers, the orders waiting to be assigned, their preparation, and what every courier has earned and worked so far. Its components are:

\begin{compactitem}
    \item the courier information $\mathcal{L}_t$, giving for each courier $c \in C$ its position $\ell_c(t)$, status $\sigma_c(t) \in \{\text{idle, picking-up, in-transit, dropping-off, offline}\}$, time to next availability $\eta_c(t)$ and on- and off-times $e_c$ and $l_c$ (the declared shift),
    \item the set $\mathcal{O}_t$ of unassigned orders; order $o$ is linked to a store $r_o \in R$ with location $\ell_{r_o}$ and to a user $u_o \in U$ with drop-off location $\ell_{u_o}$, and has placement time $a_o$, ready time $e_o$ and promised drop-off time $d_o$,
    \item the vector $\mathcal{P}_t$ of estimated remaining preparation times $\hat p_o(t)$ of the unassigned orders,
    \item and the equity history $\Hist_t = (h_c(t))_{c \in C}$, with
\end{compactitem}
\begin{equation}
h_c(t) = \bigl(\earn_c(t),\; \ord_c(t),\; \busy_c(t)\bigr), \qquad c \in C,
\label{eqn:history}
\end{equation}
the earnings, the orders delivered and the busy time (minutes in motion or serving) accumulated by courier $c$ up to $t$; the connected time $\conn_c(t) = \min(t, l_c) - e_c$ (in hours, $t$ read as clock time) follows from the declared shift recorded in $\mathcal{L}_t$, and the hourly earnings so far are $w_c(t) = \earn_c(t)/\conn_c(t)$. In summary, $S_t = (\mathcal{L}_t, \mathcal{O}_t, \mathcal{P}_t, \Hist_t)$, with $\mathcal{O}_0 = \emptyset$ and $h_c(0) = (0, 0, 0)$. The equity history is what enables dynamic fairness control, since a policy that reads $\Hist_t$ can prevent inequities from compounding across windows; it is absent from existing MDRP formulations, where the state contains the positions and plans of couriers but not what they have earned \citep{ulmer2020modeling}.

Courier $c$ has on-time $e_c$, off-time $l_c > e_c$, initial location $\ell_c$ and vehicle type $v_c \in V$, $V = \{$walking, bicycle, motorcycle, car$\}$. Travel times are deterministic, $\tau(\ell, \ell') = \kappa\, \|\ell - \ell'\| / \nu(v_c)$, with a circuity factor $\kappa$ and a vehicle-dependent speed $\nu(v_c)$; service times $s_r$ at the store and $s_u$ at the user are included in the duration of a route. A route $s$ collects at most $Q$ orders from a single store and is served by a single courier; $\dist(s)$ is its length, $\tau_s$ its duration and $|s|$ the number of orders it contains.

\paragraph{Decisions}

At each decision point $t$, the dispatcher decides which available courier receives which route and what the offer pays, a set of triples
\begin{equation}
x_t \subseteq \bigl\{(c, s, \rho_{c,s}) : c \in C_t^{\text{free}},\; s \in \mathcal{S}_t\bigr\},
\label{eqn:decision}
\end{equation}
where $C_t^{\text{free}}$ is the set of available couriers (idle, or picking up and able to receive a further offer), $\mathcal{S}_t$ the set of feasible routes that can be built from $\mathcal{O}_t$, and $\rho_{c,s}$ the pay offered for the pair, which is paid in full if the offer is accepted and not otherwise: $\pay(c, s, \rho_{c,s}) = z_{c,s}\, \rho_{c,s}$, with $z_{c,s}$ the acceptance indicator defined below. A route is feasible for a courier if every order can be picked up after its ready time and dropped off before its hard deadline $d_o + \Delta^{\max}$, given the courier's position and speed. A decision $x_t \in \mathcal{X}(S_t)$ is feasible if each courier receives at most one route and each order appears in at most one route.

The pay offer follows the platform's tariff, an affine function of route distance,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{c,s} = \rho_0 \cdot |s| + \rho_1 \cdot \dist(s),
\label{eqn:tariff}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_0$ a base fee per order and $\rho_1$ a per-kilometre rate; following the field evidence of Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description}, the baseline tariff is flat ($\rho_0 = 2{,}200$ COP, $\rho_1 = 0$). Pay-setting policies may scale the offer by a multiplier $m_c \in [1-\delta, 1+\delta]$ around the tariff, not set it arbitrarily.

\paragraph{Acceptance, exogenous information and transition}

After decision $x_t$ in state $S_t$, two kinds of information are revealed. The first is the acceptance of every offer: courier $c$ accepts route $s$ offered at pay $\rho_{c,s}$ with probability
\begin{equation}
p_{c,s}(\rho_{c,s}) =
\begin{cases}
\sigma\!\bigl(b\,[\ln(\rho_{c,s}/\dist(s)) - \ln\theta]\bigr) & \text{manual regime},\\[2pt]
1 & \text{auto-accept regime},
\end{cases}
\label{eqn:acceptance}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $\theta$ the pay per kilometre at which acceptance is even and $b$ the slope on log pay per kilometre; this is the form the field evidence supports, in which the pay decided by the platform enters the decision directly, unlike the distance-only acceptance of \cite{quintero2020computational}. We denote by $\mathbf{z}_t \in \{0,1\}^{|x_t|}$ the vector of Bernoulli acceptance realisations. The second kind of information is exogenous to the decision: $\omega_{t+1} = (\Delta\mathcal{O}_{t+1}, \Delta\mathcal{P}_{t+1}, \Delta\mathcal{A}_{t+1})$ reveals the orders placed during the window, the realised preparation times, and the courier log-ins and log-outs. The transition function $\mathcal{T}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{z}_t, \omega_{t+1})$ leads to the new state $S_{t+1}$:

\begin{compactitem}
    \item accepted routes are executed: the courier's status, position and time to availability in $\mathcal{L}_{t+1}$ follow from the route's pick-up and drop-off sequence, and the orders of accepted routes leave $\mathcal{O}_{t+1}$,
    \item the orders of rejected routes remain in $\mathcal{O}_{t+1}$, together with the new orders $\Delta\mathcal{O}_{t+1}$; orders whose hard deadline can no longer be met are cancelled,
    \item preparation estimates are replaced by realisations where revealed, $\mathcal{P}_{t+1}$,
    \item couriers that log in or out change status according to $\Delta\mathcal{A}_{t+1}$,
    \item and the equity history is updated: the pay of an accepted route is credited at acceptance, $\earn_c(t+1) = \earn_c(t) + z_{c,s}\, \rho_{c,s}$, its orders are added to $\ord_c$ as they are delivered, and $\busy_c$ accumulates the minutes the courier spends in motion or serving.
\end{compactitem}

\paragraph{Cost function}

The cost $\mathcal{C}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{z}_t, \omega_{t+1})$ of a decision penalises orders that are cancelled during the window and the lateness of orders delivered during the window,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{z}_t, \omega_{t+1}) = c_1 \sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}_t} \mathbb{I}[o \text{ cancelled}] + c_2 \sum_{o \text{ delivered}} \max\bigl(0,\; \tau_{\text{drop}}(o) - d_o\bigr),
\label{eqn:cost}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function and $c_1, c_2 > 0$ weigh coverage against punctuality. Its extension with a payment term, used by the pay-setting policy, is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{+}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{z}_t, \omega_{t+1}) = \mathcal{C}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{z}_t, \omega_{t+1}) + \lambda \sum_{(c,s) \in x_t} \pay(c, s, \rho_{c,s}),
\label{eqn:cost_plus}
\end{equation}
where $\pay(c,s,\rho_{c,s})$ is the amount paid for an accepted route and $\lambda > 0$ trades platform cost against coverage. In our experiments coverage and lateness are reported separately; the lexicographic reading coverage $\succ$ lateness used by all policies corresponds to $c_1 \gg c_2$.

\paragraph{Fairness metrics and constrained objective}

Let $w_c = \earn_c(t_{\max})/\conn_c(t_{\max})$ be the hourly earnings of courier $c$ at the end of the day, zero if it has earned nothing. The population over which fairness is measured is the set of \emph{eligible} couriers $C^{e} = \{c \in C : \conn_c(t_{\max}) \ge 1 \text{ h}\}$, fixed by the couriers' connection decisions and not by the dispatcher. The primary fairness metric is the Gini coefficient of hourly earnings over $C^{e}$,
\begin{equation}
\FG(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\sum_{c \in C^{e}} \sum_{c' \in C^{e}} |w_c - w_{c'}|}{2\,|C^{e}| \sum_{c \in C^{e}} w_c},
\qquad \mathbf{w} = (w_c)_{c \in C^{e}},
\label{eqn:gini}
\end{equation}
with $\FG \in [0,1]$ and $0$ perfect equality. Normalising by connected time makes Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini} an exposure-adjusted statistic, and the two restrictions on the population make it a guarantee: a policy cannot lower it by leaving couriers idle, since they enter with $w_c = 0$, nor by dispatching single orders to couriers who connect for a few minutes, since they are excluded. The same statistic over the couriers who delivered at least one order, $\FGa$, is the one used by the fairness literature of Section~\ref{sec:Literature}; it can be lowered by both manoeuvres, and Section~\ref{sec:results_quality} shows that it is. Three secondary metrics are reported over the active couriers: max--min fairness $\FM = \min_c w_c$, order-count balance $\FO$, the Gini coefficient of orders delivered per connected hour, and the minimum-wage deficit $\FD$, the share of active couriers below the statutory minimum hourly wage $w^*$.

A solution is a policy $\pi \in \Pi$ that assigns a decision $\mathcal{X}^\pi_t(S_t)$ to each state; the optimal policy minimises the expected cumulative cost subject to the end-of-day guarantee:
\begin{alignat}{2}
\pi^{*} = \underset{\pi \in \Pi}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;& \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t \in T} \mathcal{C}\bigl(S_t, \mathcal{X}^\pi_t(S_t), \mathbf{z}_t, \omega_{t+1}\bigr) \,\Big|\, S_0 \right] \label{eqn:objective}\\
\text{s.t.}\;\; & \mathbb{E}\bigl[\FG(\mathbf{w}^{\pi}) \,\big|\, S_0\bigr] \le \alpha, \label{eqn:fair_const}\\
& \mathcal{X}^\pi_t(S_t) \in \mathcal{X}(S_t) \qquad \forall t \in T, \label{eqn:feasibility}
\end{alignat}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is the tolerated inequality and $\mathbf{w}^{\pi}$ the end-of-day hourly earnings induced by $\pi$; the day is finite, so no discounting is applied. Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} is deliberately written on an end-of-day quantity, not per decision point, which is what the equity history makes controllable; in the computational study we report, besides the mean of $\FG$, the fraction of realisations in which $\FG \le \alpha$ holds, which is the chance-constrained reading $\mathbb{P}(\FG \le \alpha) \ge 1 - \beta$ of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const}; we report it for every $\alpha$ of the grid rather than fixing $\beta$. Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} constrains a terminal, non-linear functional of the whole trajectory rather than an expected cumulative cost, so the Lagrangian machinery of constrained MDPs \citep{altman1999constrained,borkar2005actor} does not apply directly; the policies of Section~\ref{sec:method} are heuristics for either reading. A minimum-wage constraint $\mathbb{E}[w_c^{\pi}] \ge w^*$ is not imposed because 92\% of couriers earn below $w^*$ in the field data, so it would be infeasible on every instance; it is pursued through $\FD$ by the pay-setting policy (Section~\ref{sec:paybrkga}) and reported for every policy.

The formulation relates to three familiar models: with $\alpha = 1$, perfect acceptance and tariff pay, Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:objective}--\eqref{eqn:feasibility} are the corporate, efficiency-only MDRP of \cite{reyes2018meal}; the constraint slot of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} admits $\FO$ in place of $\FG$, the balance criterion of \cite{wen2010dynamic} and \cite{liao2020multi}; and a bound on $\FD$, or the minimum-wage constraint alone, is the wage criterion of \cite{nair2022work4food}, whose levers (fleet size and paid idle time) this model does not have. Since the vehicle type of every courier is recorded, the emissions of a decision, $\text{Em}(x_t) = \sum_{(c,s) \in x_t} z_{c,s}\, \epsilon(v_c) \dist(s)$ with $\epsilon(v)$ a per-kilometre emission factor (zero for walking and bicycles), are a diagnostic reported alongside the frontiers, not a constraint.

Even in the two-courier example of \ref{sec:example} the challenge becomes evident: a policy that equalises the pay offered within every window can end the day with a Gini coefficient of $0.5$ between two equally connected couriers where $0$ was attainable at the same coverage, because the per-window statistic ignores what each courier has already earned. A policy therefore needs $\Hist_t$ to control Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const}; the next section presents three policies that read it.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

In this section, we present our policies. We first give a motivation and overview of the shared architecture and then present the individual policies in detail.

\subsection{Motivation and overview}
\label{sec:motivation_method}

Effective decision making is challenging for two reasons.

\begin{compactitem}
    \item \textbf{Search:} A round decides, for up to a few hundred free couriers in a zone (two thousand on the largest Bogot\'a day) and a few hundred ready orders, which courier receives which candidate route and what the offer pays, within the two-minute window.
    \item \textbf{Evaluate:} The value of a decision has two components that are revealed at different times. Whether a route is accepted is revealed within seconds and depends on the pay; whether the end-of-day guarantee is met is revealed only at the end of the day and depends on every subsequent round. The second component cannot be evaluated per window: as the example of \ref{sec:example} shows, two decisions that are indistinguishable by any statistic of the current window can lead to end-of-day Gini coefficients of $0$ and $0.5$.
\end{compactitem}

We address these challenges as follows. All policies, proposed and benchmark, are implemented as the assignment step of one discrete-event simulator and share (i) a round every two minutes; (ii) the same candidate routes, every singleton and one bundle per store with its $Q$ most urgent ready orders, a generator under which bundling is nearly switched off (mean bundle size 1.00--1.03, Section~\ref{sec:results_decisions}), and candidate pairs built in the same way: for each route, the $k$ available couriers nearest to its store in straight-line distance that can feasibly serve it ($k = 25$ for every policy except \textbf{MIP-Fair}, which uses $k = 8$ for budget reasons, Section~\ref{sec:implementation}) plus, when the deficit-aware prefilter is on, the $k_d$ available couriers with the largest positive deficit outside that set, the nearest among ties, again subject to feasibility; (iii) the acceptance model of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance} and the flat tariff; and (iv) the same metrics, computed on the end-of-day history $\Hist_{t_{\max}}$. Let $K_t$ denote the set of candidate (courier, route) pairs at round $t$. Each policy returns a subset of $K_t$ with at most one route per courier and one route per order, together with a pay offer per pair, that is, a feasible decision $x_t$ in the sense of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:decision}.

The three policies differ in how they use $\Hist_t$ to steer the current round towards the end-of-day guarantee, and in which lever they move. \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} keeps the population and fitness of a coverage-maximising genetic algorithm and biases its decoder: among decodings of equal coverage, routes are redirected to couriers whose hourly earnings so far fall below the mean, by a weight $\gamma$. \textbf{MIP-Fair} reads the constraint literally: in every round it solves a mixed-integer programme that maximises coverage first and then minimises the amount by which the projected end-of-round Gini coefficient exceeds $\alpha$, with the constraint linearised exactly. \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} moves the other lever: it keeps the decoder of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} without the deficit bias and adds one pay gene per courier, so that the offer can rise for couriers below the minimum wage and fall where acceptance is not at stake, within a band around the tariff; its fitness trades expected coverage, projected minimum-wage deficit and pay excess with a weight $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$. As cost function approximations (Section~\ref{sec:Literature}), the three modify the per-round problem by terms computed from the state rather than by a learned value of the future. The parameters $\gamma$, $\alpha$ and $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ are tuned by enumeration, since their frontier is what the platform needs to see. Algorithm~\ref{alg:round} summarises a round for the three policies; the following sections detail each component.

We call a dispatching rule \emph{courier-centric} when each round visits the free couriers in a given order and lets each take its best feasible route, and \emph{order-centric} when each round chooses the assignment jointly to serve the ready orders best (coverage first, then lateness), so that a courier receives a route only if it is among the best placed for some order. The two greedy benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:Benchmark}) are courier-centric: \textbf{Greedy} visits the least-served couriers first, which is a reading of one component of $\Hist_t$, $\ord_c$, and \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} visits them in order of log-in, which reads nothing of $\Hist_t$; the \textbf{ILP}, \textbf{Hierarchical}, the BRKGA family and \textbf{MIP-Fair} are order-centric, whatever they read from $\Hist_t$.

\paragraph{Content and population of the history} Each policy reads $\Hist_t$ through a courier value and a population, and we evaluate two readings. The \emph{activity basis} (the first generation of Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}) values a courier by its earnings per busy hour, $\earn_c(t)/\busy_c(t)$, over the couriers of the round; the \emph{exposure basis} (second generation) uses the earnings per connected hour $w_c(t) = \earn_c(t)/\conn_c(t)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}, over the couriers whose declared shift is at least one hour, the eligible population as it is known at log-in, and switches on the deficit-aware prefilter with $k_d \in \{8, 25, 50\}$. We call the first-generation configuration from which a second-generation variant descends its \emph{parent}. The deficit of a courier that has earned nothing is one under either basis, however long it has waited, so the prefilter puts the nearest idle couriers in front of the solver; the basis matters for the couriers with earnings, whom the activity basis rates by productivity and the exposure basis by what they have earned over their connected time.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\FOR{$t = 0, \dots, t_{\max}$}
\STATE{build the candidate routes $\mathcal{S}_t$ (singletons and one bundle per store) from the ready orders in $\mathcal{O}_t$}
\STATE{build the candidate pairs $K_t$: for each route, the $k$ nearest available couriers that can feasibly serve it, plus the $k_d$ available couriers with the largest positive deficit outside that set ($k_d = 0$ in the first generation)}
\STATE{compute from $\Hist_t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eqn:history}) the value $w_c(t)$ of every connected courier under the basis in use (earnings per busy hour in the first generation, per connected hour in the second)}
\IF{\textbf{BRKGA-Fair} or \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}}
\STATE{run the BRKGA for $G = 10$ generations with decoder of Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder} and score of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:brkgafair} ($\gamma = 0$, and fitness of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:paybrkga}, for \textbf{Pay-BRKGA})}
\ELSIF{\textbf{MIP-Fair}}
\STATE{solve the three-stage programme of Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_w}--\eqref{eqn:mipfair_lex} over $K_t$}
\ENDIF
\STATE{offer the selected pairs with pay $\rho_{c,s}$; draw acceptance from Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance}}
\STATE{execute accepted routes, return rejected orders to the pool, update $\Hist_{t+1}$}
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{Dispatching round shared by the three proposed policies}
\label{alg:round}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Detailed description of the policies}

In this section, we provide details of every component. We first describe the coverage-maximising BRKGA that \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} extend, then the three policies, and finally their verification.

\paragraph{Coverage BRKGA and decoder} The biased random-key genetic algorithm \citep{goncalves2011biased} with rolling horizon is ported from our earlier work on the MDRP \citep{giraldo2026brkga}, where it was evaluated on full-city days of the same dataset against two rolling-horizon heuristics, with and without demand look-ahead. The chromosome has one key per ready order and one per available courier. The decoder (Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder} in \ref{sec:App_decoder}) visits stores in the order of their highest-key order; at each store it takes the head order, evaluates its candidate routes (singleton and bundle) against the $K^{\text{dec}}$ highest-key couriers among those that can feasibly serve the route, and keeps the pair with the best score. Fitness is coverage, with mean travel time as a tie-breaker. The decoder score is the finishing time per order, $\text{score}_{\text{fpo}}(s, c) = \text{score}_{\text{finish}}(s, c)/|s|$ with $\text{score}_{\text{finish}}$ the time at which the courier finishes the route, so that bundles compete with singletons; the original finishing-time score, under which a bundle never beats the singleton with the same head order, is an ablation in \ref{sec:App_ablations}. The decoder selects the $K^{\text{dec}}$ couriers among the candidate pairs of the route.

\paragraph{BRKGA-Fair}
\label{sec:brkgafair}
\textbf{BRKGA-Fair} is the coverage BRKGA with a decoder that reads the equity deficit from $\Hist_t$. Only the score changes:
\begin{equation}
\text{score}_{\text{fair}}(s, c) = \text{score}(s, c) - \gamma \cdot \text{deficit}_c, \qquad
\text{deficit}_c = \frac{\max(0,\; \bar w_t - w_c(t))}{\bar w_t} \in [0,1],
\label{eqn:brkgafair}
\end{equation}
where $w_c(t)$ is the courier's value under the basis in use (zero if it has earned nothing), $\bar w_t$ is the mean over the free couriers of the round, and $\gamma$ is a weight in minutes per order: a courier with full deficit is treated as if every route of theirs finished $\gamma$ minutes per order earlier under $\text{score}_{\text{fpo}}$. The fitness that ranks the population is unchanged, so the deficit redirects routes among decodings of equal coverage and the population retains its maximum-coverage members; the deficit acts only among the $K^{\text{dec}}$ couriers that the decoder evaluates for each route, which bounds its effect by construction. With $\gamma = 0$ the policy is identical to the coverage BRKGA for the same seed, which isolates the value of the deficit term. A total-earnings basis would penalise connecting late rather than being under-served, and is reported as an ablation.

\paragraph{MIP-Fair}
\label{sec:mipfair}
\textbf{MIP-Fair} solves, at each round, a mixed-integer programme over $K_t$ with the Gini constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} written on the projected end-of-round values of $\Hist_t$. Let $x_{cs} \in \{0,1\}$ indicate that pair $(c,s) \in K_t$ is dispatched and $y_o \in \{0,1\}$ that order $o$ is covered. Because a courier takes at most one route per round, the projected hourly earnings of a free courier are linear in $x$:
\begin{equation}
w_c = w_c^0 \Bigl(1 - \sum_{s} x_{cs}\Bigr) + \sum_{s} x_{cs}\, w_c(s), \qquad
w_c(s) = \frac{\earn_c(t) + \rho_{c,s}}{(\tau^{\text{base}}_c(t) + \tau_s)/60},
\label{eqn:mipfair_w}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_s$ is the route's travel-plus-service time in minutes, $\tau^{\text{base}}_c(t)$ is $\busy_c(t)$ under the activity basis and $\conn_c(t)$ under the exposure basis, and the projection assumes that the offer is accepted. Couriers that are busy, or connected for less than a short floor, enter with fixed values $w_c = w_c^0$. The Gini constraint is evaluated over all connected couriers, not only the free ones of the round, because Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} is over $C^{e}$ and the free couriers are a biased subset; the free-only scope is reported as an ablation and turns out to be tighter but eight times more expensive (\ref{sec:App_ablations}). The two readings of Section~\ref{sec:motivation_method} apply here as to the deficit of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}; \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} reads the deficit only through the prefilter. Writing $n = |C_t|$ for the number of connected couriers, the constraint $\FG(\mathbf{w}) \le \alpha$ is equivalent to $\sum_{i<j} |w_i - w_j| - \alpha\, n \sum_c w_c \le 0$, and since the inequality is in the direction that allows it, it is linearised exactly as
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i<j} d_{ij} - \alpha\, n \sum_c w_c \;\le\; n\, W_{\text{ref}}\, v, \qquad
d_{ij} \ge w_i - w_j,\;\; d_{ij} \ge w_j - w_i,\;\; v \ge 0,
\label{eqn:mipfair_gini}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is an elastic \enquote{Gini excess}, the excess of the sum of absolute differences over $\alpha\, n \sum_c w_c$ scaled by $n W_{\text{ref}}$ so that no round is infeasible, and $v^* > 0$ measures by how much $\alpha$ could not be met in that round. Building $d_{ij}$ for all pairs is $O(n^2)$ per round, which is prohibitive with thousands of connected couriers (\ref{sec:app_MIP}). The pairs are therefore handled in three blocks. Fixed--fixed pairs contribute a constant. Free--fixed pairs are linear in $x$, because the free courier's $w_i$ takes one of finitely many values ($w_i^0$ or $w_i(s)$), so $|w_i - w_j| = (1 - \sum_s x_{is})\,|w_i^0 - w_j| + \sum_s x_{is}\,|w_i(s) - w_j|$. Only free--free pairs require the variables $d_{ij}$. The linearisation is exact for the projected values, which we verify against the all-pairs model on a test instance and on 117 real rounds of two Pereira days, where the two models reach the same coverage and Gini-excess optima in every round with 78--105 pair variables instead of 7{,}381--14{,}028 (\ref{sec:App_verification}); what is approximate is the projection itself: it assumes acceptance, it is taken over the couriers connected at $t$ rather than over the end-of-day population, the denominator of a dispatched courier advances by $\tau_s$ while the others' do not, and $\alpha \to 0$ minimises the sum of absolute differences of the projected values rather than their Gini coefficient, since the denominator $\sum_c w_c$ varies with $x$. The programme is solved lexicographically in three stages, each fixing the previous optimum:
\begin{equation}
\max \sum_o y_o \;\succ\; \min v \;\succ\; \min \Bigl(\lambda_{\text{tt}} \sum_{(c,s)} \tau_s x_{cs} + \mu \sum_o \text{late}_o\Bigr),
\label{eqn:mipfair_lex}
\end{equation}
subject to each order in at most one dispatched route, each courier in at most one, $y_o \le \sum_{(c,s) \ni o} x_{cs}$, and Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_w}--\eqref{eqn:mipfair_gini}. The complete formulation is given in \ref{sec:app_MIP}. Coverage is placed first because the alternative order (equity first) collapses from a cold start (\ref{sec:app_MIP}). With coverage first, the Gini excess decides \emph{who} receives each route among the maximum-coverage assignments, which is the equity lever; $\alpha = 1$ switches the constraint off and leaves a coverage programme that differs from the \textbf{ILP} only in its tie-breaking stage (travel time and lateness against travel time alone), and $\alpha \to 0$ minimises the projected absolute differences in every round. The equity-first variant is reported as an ablation, and so is a coverage slack $\varepsilon$ that lets the later stages give up a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the stage-1 coverage ($\varepsilon = 0$ otherwise).

\paragraph{Pay-BRKGA}
\label{sec:paybrkga}
\textbf{Pay-BRKGA} decides who is paid how much, not how routes are built. The chromosome of the coverage BRKGA is extended with one pay gene $g_c \in [0, 1]$ per free courier, mapped to the multiplier $m_c = (1 - \delta) + 2\delta g_c \in [1-\delta, 1+\delta]$ of whatever route the decoder assigns to $c$, and the offer is $\rho_{c,s} = \rho_0 \cdot |s| \cdot m_c$; under the affine tariff of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:tariff} the multiplier would apply to $\rho_1$ only. The decoder is Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder}. The fitness is the scalarised objective, in coverage-fraction units and to be minimised,
\begin{equation}
\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{covered}]}{|\mathcal{O}_t|}\Bigr)
+ \lambda_{\text{def}}\, \FD^{\text{proj}}
+ \lambda_{\text{pay}} \Bigl(\frac{\sum \pay}{\sum \text{tariff}} - 1\Bigr)
+ 10^{-3}\,\frac{\bar\tau}{20},
\label{eqn:paybrkga}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{E}[\text{covered}] = \sum_{(c,s) \in A} |s|\; p_{c,s}(\rho_{c,s})$ uses the acceptance model of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance}, so that raising pay buys coverage only if the behavioural model says it does (in the auto-accept regime pay moves only $\FD$ and cost); $\FD^{\text{proj}}$ is the fraction of free couriers whose projected hourly earnings after the round, assuming acceptance, remain below $w^*$; the third term is the relative pay excess over the tariff, the payment term of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:cost_plus} normalised by the tariff; and the last term breaks ties by mean travel time $\bar\tau$. On these data $\FD^{\text{proj}}$ is close to one for every chromosome, because no courier reaches $w^*$ within a round (Section~\ref{sec:results_decisions}), so the term is inert at $\lambda_{\text{def}} = 0.5$ and the fitness trades, in practice, expected coverage against pay. The scalarisation is a weighted sum rather than an explicit Pareto set because a round yields one decision; sweeping $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ traces the cost-of-fairness curve. We note that the mechanism \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} acts on is distance, not pay: under the flat tariff, a 2-km singleton pays 1{,}100 COP/km (acceptance about 0.64 at $\theta = 903$) while a 4-km one pays 550 COP/km (about 0.19), so the pay gene is the only lever that can make a long route acceptable in the manual regime.

\paragraph{Verification} The four pieces are covered by unit tests against exhaustive enumeration, and the block linearisation is verified against the all-pairs model (\ref{sec:App_verification}). In summary, the three policies differ in the lever they move and in the solver, and share the candidate pairs and the two readings of the history.

\section{Experimental setting}
\label{sec:Experimental_setting}

In the following, we outline the instances, the benchmark policies, the experimental design and the implementation details of our computational study.

\subsection{Instances}
\label{sec:instances}

We test our policies on 24 full operating days (00:00--24:00) of a Colombian on-demand delivery platform, made public by \citet{quintero2020computational}. Each day records every order (store, drop-off coordinates, placement, preparation and promised drop-off times) and every courier (vehicle, connection point, on and off times). Projecting the coordinates showed that the 24 days cover six cities: Bogot\'a, Medell\'in, Cali, Barranquilla, Bucaramanga and Pereira (four days each). Full-city days range from 540 orders and 400 couriers (Pereira) to 59{,}000 orders and 16{,}800 couriers (Bogot\'a); fleet composition differs sharply, from 45\% bicycles in Bogot\'a to 90\% motorcycles in Pereira and Bucaramanga, which matters for the emissions diagnostic. The behavioural parameters come from the interviews and the survey described in Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description}; the remaining parameters are either platform practice or declared assumptions. The simulator, the policies, the instance loader and the batch scripts are available at \url{https://github.com/Danielgiraldosb/mdrp-simulator}.

\paragraph{Layout} A full city day is not the problem a dispatcher solves, so the experimental unit is a \emph{zone}: the orders whose store and drop-off both lie within 3 km of the instance's median store location, and the couriers whose connection point does, over the full day. Three kilometres keeps Pereira above a few hundred orders, so that end-of-day Gini coefficients are not noise, and Bogot\'a below the scale at which the per-round MIP exceeds the budget. Table~\ref{tab:instances} in \ref{sec:App_instances} lists the 24 instances; zone sizes range from 342 to 10{,}215 orders (mean 3{,}609), and Figure~\ref{fig:map} in \ref{sec:App_instances} shows the zone of instance 0 in Bogot\'a with its stores, drop-offs and courier connection points. Two properties of the instances follow the comparisons throughout. First, couriers outnumber the work: the zones have 0.40 orders per connected courier-hour and the full city days 0.36, so the ratio is a property of the connection logs and not of the zone, and surveyed couriers report a similar 0.35 outside the bonus ladder and 1.15 inside it (medians of orders per day over hours connected per working day); Section~\ref{sec:results_sensitivity} reruns \textbf{Greedy} and \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} in a sensitivity scenario with the fleet thinned to one courier in three, which brings the zones to about the in-ladder ratio. Second, about a fifth of the orders cannot be delivered on time by any courier of the zone, which bounds coverage near 0.79 for every policy.

\paragraph{Couriers and travel} Travel time is Euclidean distance times a circuity factor of $\kappa = 1.3$, standard for an urban street network relative to the straight line (1.0 in a sensitivity run), divided by the vehicle speed: 15 km/h for bicycles, 25 for motorcycles, 5 for walking and 20 for cars, declared as assumptions of the same order as the 320 m/min of the public MDRP instances \citep{reyes2018meal}. Couriers connect and disconnect at the recorded on and off times; repositioning of idle couriers is switched off because most surveyed couriers wait in place. A courier decides on an offer after a normally distributed delay with mean 16 s and standard deviation 8 s, truncated at 3 s.

\paragraph{Orders and service} In-store service takes 20 minutes with a purchased order (60 minutes in a stress run) and drop-off service 4 minutes, the default of the public instances. The hard deadline is the promised drop-off time plus $\Delta^{\max} = 30$ minutes, the slack the interviews describe as tolerated. Route capacity is $Q = 3$. Customer cancellations are switched off because there is no field evidence on them.

\paragraph{Tariff, acceptance and wage} The flat tariff pays $\rho_0 = 2{,}200$ COP per order (survey, $n = 132$); the pay band of \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} is $\delta = 0.30$. The logit of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance} uses $\theta = 903$ COP/km and $b = 2.89$, the model estimated on 32 decisions by 11 interviewees, 31 of them from 2020, when offers were selected manually. Figure~\ref{fig:acceptance} in \ref{sec:App_instances} shows the curve with its interval and the two regimes; the full estimation is in the online supplement. The statutory minimum hourly wage is $w^* = 4{,}367.91$ COP: the 2021 monthly minimum of 908{,}526 COP \citep{colombia2021smmlv} divided by 208 hours (48 hours a week $\times$ 52/12 weeks). Emission factors are 0.114 kg CO$_2$e/km for motorcycles and 0.167 for cars \citep{defra2023factors}, zero for bicycles and walking; local factors by vehicle type do not exist. Table~\ref{tab:parameters} in \ref{sec:App_instances} summarises the parameters with their sources and, for the behavioural ones, the evidence grade.

\subsection{Benchmark policies}
\label{sec:Benchmark}

We compare the three proposed policies against five benchmarks designed to isolate the value of the equity history, of the exact constraint and of the pay lever.

\begin{compactitem}
    \item \textbf{Greedy}: Courier-centric. The free couriers are visited in increasing order of the orders they have delivered so far, and each takes the best feasible route (a bundle when one is feasible, otherwise the nearest order) at its nearest store with pending orders. The couriers describe the platform's dispatch as \enquote{the free couriers pick work in turn}, but the platform does not disclose its queue rule and neither the interviews nor the survey identifies it, so \textbf{Greedy} is not a model of current practice: it is a courier-centric benchmark with an equity priority, the least-served-first order, which reads one number of $\Hist_t$, the order count, as a priority rule. It is the reference for all paired comparisons, and its end-of-day Gini coefficient is the courier-centric anchor for $\alpha$ (Section~\ref{sec:results_sensitivity}).

    \item \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}: The same courier-centric dispatcher with the free couriers visited in order of log-in, first logged in first served, so that it reads nothing of $\Hist_t$. It isolates the equity priority of \textbf{Greedy}.

    \item \textbf{ILP}: A per-round integer programme over $K_t$ that maximises the number of orders covered, with a secondary term for travel time. It is the exact per-window coverage dispatcher and the model that \textbf{MIP-Fair} extends; the two differ in the candidate prefilter ($k = 25$ against $k = 8$, Section~\ref{sec:implementation}), so the comparison that isolates the Gini constraint is \textbf{MIP-Fair} at $\alpha = 1$ against $\alpha < 1$ (Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}); Section~\ref{sec:results_sensitivity} also runs the \textbf{ILP} with $k = 8$.

    \item \textbf{BRKGA}: The coverage BRKGA of Section~\ref{sec:method} with $\gamma = 0$ and the tariff pay. Comparing it with \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} isolates the deficit term, and with \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} the pay gene.

    \item \textbf{Hierarchical}: A per-round lexicographic policy that first maximises coverage, then minimises lateness, then balances the pay offered among the free couriers of the round, then minimises cost. It is fair within each window and ignores $\Hist_t$; it is the empirical counterpart of the example in \ref{sec:example}, and comparing it with \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} isolates the value of carrying the history in the state.
\end{compactitem}

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

Table~\ref{tab:factors} in \ref{sec:App_instances} lists the factors and levels. Eighteen policy configurations (the four benchmarks other than \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, four weights $\gamma$ of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, six thresholds of \textbf{MIP-Fair} and four pay weights of \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}) are crossed with the two acceptance regimes, the 24 instances and ten seeds. Seeds are common random numbers (1 to 10): every comparison between policies is paired by (instance, seed), and the seed drives the acceptance draws, the decision delays and the BRKGA. The $\alpha$ grid is placed around the survey anchors of Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description} (0.28 and 0.41).

The study consists of four experiments. \textbf{X0}, the pilot, runs six instances (five cities) for the seven policies other than \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} at default settings, manual regime and one seed (42 runs) to measure run times and the end-of-day Gini coefficient of \textbf{Greedy}. \textbf{X1}, the main experiment, runs the 18 configurations for both regimes on the 24 instances with ten seeds (8{,}640 runs) and produces the frontiers, the cost of the guarantee, the compliance and the regime loss. \textbf{X2}, the threshold experiment, re-runs six selected configurations (\textbf{Greedy}, \textbf{Hierarchical}, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with $\gamma = 40$ and $k_d = 25$ or $50$, \textbf{MIP-Fair} with $\alpha = 0.30$ and $k_d = 25$, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} with $\lambda_{\text{pay}} = 0.5$) at the bounds of the threshold's confidence interval under the manual regime, the only one in which the threshold is read, with five seeds (1{,}440 runs, 1{,}437 completed). \textbf{X3} evaluates, with five seeds, the second-generation variants of Section~\ref{sec:results_quality} (exposure basis, deficit-aware prefilter with $k_d \in \{8, 25\}$ and coverage slack; eight configurations under both regimes, 1{,}920 runs) and a supplement of eleven ablations (manual regime except $k_d = 50$, 1{,}453 runs completed; \ref{sec:App_ablations}). A review experiment adds the \textbf{ILP} with $k = 8$ and \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} (the ten seeds of X1, both regimes) and three policies with the fleet thinned to one third (five seeds, both regimes), 1{,}680 runs. The total computational effort is reported in \ref{sec:App_instances} of the supplementary material.

\subsection{Metrics and analysis}
\label{sec:metrics}

For every run we record coverage, lateness, click-to-door times, the end-of-day $\FG$, $\FM$, $\FO$ and $\FD$, payment, the active couriers, the bundle size, the solve times and emissions. The reported Gini coefficient is one and the same for all policies, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini} over the eligible couriers, computed ex post; $\FGa$ is reported beside it. We report the mean over the 24 instances (seeds averaged within) with its standard error, the cost of the guarantee as the paired difference with \textbf{Greedy} with a 95\% bootstrap interval, the regime loss as the paired difference manual minus auto-accept, and compliance as the fraction of (instance, seed) pairs, which we call days for short, with $\FG \le \alpha$. The full statistical procedure, including the share of the gap closed and the frontiers, is in \ref{sec:App_instances} of the supplementary material.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{sec:implementation}

The parameters of the policies were tuned in preliminary experiments. The BRKGA uses a population of 10 and 10 generations per round, elite and mutant fractions of 20\%, elite bias 0.7 and $K^{\text{dec}} = 3$ couriers per order in the decoder; a population of 20 with 20 generations is an ablation. The candidate prefilter keeps the $k = 25$ nearest available couriers per route for all policies except \textbf{MIP-Fair}, which uses $k = 8$ for budget reasons (\ref{sec:app_tuning}); $k = 25$ is an ablation. \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} uses $\lambda_{\text{def}} = 0.5$. Mixed-integer programmes are solved with a limit of 10 seconds per stage, each lexicographic stage being a separate solve, and each run has a timeout of 90 minutes in X1, 150 in X2 and in the second generation of X3, and 240 in the ablations of X3. All experiments were conducted on a computer equipped with an Intel Core i9-14900K processor with turbo disabled, running Windows 10, with 12 parallel processes; the CPU times reported are wall-clock minutes per run under that load, so ratios between policies are comparable and absolute values conservative. The implementation was carried out in Python 3.10, and Gurobi 11.0 was used for the \textbf{ILP}, \textbf{MIP-Fair} and \textbf{Hierarchical} policies.

\section{Computational study}
\label{sec:computational_study}

In this section, we present our computational study. We first compare the policies with respect to coverage, punctuality and the end-of-day Gini coefficient, and report whether they comply with the guarantee. Then, we perform an in-depth analysis of the equity history: how the Gini coefficient evolves within the day under the per-window policy and under the policies that read $\Hist_t$, and how the three policies change who receives which route and what it pays. Finally, we examine how policy performance changes across operational settings: the acceptance regime, the behavioural threshold, the tolerated inequality and the fleet composition of the six cities.

Three facts organise the table. First, under the auto-accept regime coverage is identical for the 18 configurations and \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, $0.786 \pm 0.013$: the orders that are not delivered (21\%) cannot be delivered on time by any courier of the zone, so the fleet is not binding and the policies differ only in who delivers, when, and at what pay. Under the manual regime rejection opens a spread in coverage, from $0.737$ (\textbf{ILP}) to $0.770$ (\textbf{BRKGA-Fair}). Second, \textbf{Greedy} is dominated on the service side: every order-centric policy except \textbf{ILP} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} delivers at least as much with less lateness ($-0.9$ to $-1.8$ minutes under manual acceptance and $-0.3$ to $-1.1$ under auto-accept, paired intervals excluding zero). \textbf{ILP} covers least under manual acceptance because of its tie-breaking stage, not its candidate set (Section~\ref{sec:results_sensitivity}). Third, and this is the central result of the paper, \textbf{Greedy} is the most equitable policy by a wide margin on the statistic of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}: $0.304$ under the manual regime and $0.299$ under auto-accept, against $0.501$--$0.565$ for every order-centric policy, the three proposed ones included, and $0.744$--$0.751$ for \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}. The paired differences of the order-centric policies with respect to \textbf{Greedy} are between $+0.202$ and $+0.267$, every 95\% interval at least $0.17$ from zero, and the ranking holds in each of the six cities (Table~\ref{tab:city}) and with eligibility thresholds of 0.5 and 2 hours instead of one (Table~\ref{tab:eligibility} in \ref{sec:App_population}) and, on average, over the couriers connected during the zone's busiest hour, with earnings per hour or per order placed while connected (Table~\ref{tab:alt_population} in \ref{sec:App_objections}); it narrows or reverses only when every courier enters, including those connected for a few minutes.

The mechanism is who gets work. \textbf{Greedy} is courier-centric: every free courier takes its best route, the least-served courier first, so 97\% of the eligible couriers deliver at least one order during the day; \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} visits them in order of log-in, so the first to connect are first in every round and only 38\% of the eligible couriers deliver. The exact \textbf{ILP}, \textbf{MIP-Fair}, the BRKGA family and \textbf{Hierarchical} are order-centric: each round assigns the orders to the best-placed couriers, the same couriers tend to be best placed round after round, and between 21\% and 30\% of the eligible couriers end the day without a single delivery and with $w_c = 0$. The first quartile of hourly earnings among eligible couriers is $481$ COP under \textbf{Greedy}, between $86$ and $146$ COP under the order-centric policies and zero under \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}. This inverts the statistic that the fairness literature, and the first version of this study, computed over the active couriers: among the policies of X1, $\FGa$ is lowest for \textbf{ILP} ($0.411$) and highest for \textbf{Greedy} ($0.514$) and \textbf{Hierarchical} ($0.530$), and lowest of all for \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} ($0.339$), the least equitable policy on Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}, because idle couriers leave the active population and the couriers who connect for less than one hour enter it; Table~\ref{tab:scope} in \ref{sec:App_population} decomposes the two manoeuvres.

The equity history did not prevent this because of what the three policies read in it. The deficit of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and the projected $w_c$ of \textbf{MIP-Fair} are computed per \emph{busy} hour, a productivity rate: a courier with one ten-minute delivery and a courier with twenty have nearly the same value, and only a courier who has never delivered has a deficit. The signal therefore separates couriers with and without work but not couriers with little and much, and it competes with coverage, which has lexicographic priority in \textbf{MIP-Fair} and is the fitness of the BRKGA population. The deficit bias does act in the right direction: \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} raises the active share of eligible couriers from $0.70$ (\textbf{BRKGA}) to $0.76$ and lowers $\FG$ from $0.564$--$0.565$ to $0.533$--$0.546$ as $\gamma$ grows from $5$ to $40$, in both regimes, and \textbf{MIP-Fair} activates more couriers than \textbf{BRKGA}; but the effect is a fraction of the gap to \textbf{Greedy}. The $\alpha$ of \textbf{MIP-Fair} has no measurable effect ($0.542$--$0.543$ under the manual regime, including $\alpha = 1.00$, which switches the constraint off), because with coverage first and $k = 8$ candidates the coverage-optimal assignments of a round leave the Gini block almost nothing to choose. The second-generation variants that correct this are evaluated in the next paragraph. The minimum-wage deficit is between $0.974$ and $0.994$ under every policy of X1 and $1.000$ under \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, consistent with the 92\% of surveyed couriers below the statutory minimum.
\paragraph{Second-generation variants} Two changes follow from this diagnosis. First, the history $\Hist_t$ that the three policies read is the \emph{exposure} basis of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}, earnings per hour connected so far over the eligible couriers, so that a courier with few deliveries over a long connection carries a large deficit instead of looking productive. Second, the deficit-aware prefilter adds to the candidate pairs of each route, after its $k$ nearest available couriers, the $k_d$ available couriers with the largest deficit, the nearest among ties, so that the idle couriers, whose deficit is one, are at least offered to the solver. Table~\ref{tab:gen2} in \ref{sec:App_gen2} of the supplementary material reports the variants on the five seeds shared with X1 against their first-generation parents and \textbf{Greedy}, and Table~\ref{tab:gen2_paired} the paired differences with their intervals, the share of the gap closed and the compliance of every variant. The basis alone is almost inert: it moves $\FG$ by $0.004$--$0.009$ (\textbf{MIP-Fair}) and $0.023$--$0.025$ (\textbf{BRKGA-Fair}) and closes 2--11\% of the gap to \textbf{Greedy}, because an idle courier who is not a candidate cannot be chosen however large its deficit. The prefilter is the lever, and its effect grows with $k_d$: with $k_d = 8$ the gap closes by 23--24\% (\textbf{BRKGA-Fair}) and 32--45\% (\textbf{MIP-Fair}); with $k_d = 25$ by 42--51\% (\textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, $\gamma = 40$) and 45--56\% (\textbf{MIP-Fair}), to $\FG = 0.435$--$0.436$ under the manual regime and $0.401$--$0.415$ under auto-accept, with 85--91\% of the eligible couriers active. Every paired interval excludes zero (Table~\ref{tab:gen2_paired}), and no (instance, seed) pair is worse than its parent at $k_d = 25$. The service cost is nil: coverage changes by $-0.001$ to $+0.003$ with respect to the parent (the \textbf{MIP-Fair} variants gain slightly, because the extra candidates also serve coverage), lateness rises by at most $0.3$ minutes ($0.1$ under auto-accept), and the regime loss is unchanged. Half of the gap to \textbf{Greedy} remains; the CPU cost of $k_d$, which makes the heuristic the practical choice at this scale, the coverage slack, the weaker response of \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} and the ablation with $k_d = 50$ are discussed in \ref{sec:App_gen2}.

Table~\ref{tab:compliance} in \ref{sec:App_population} reports the compliance with the guarantee, the fraction of (instance, seed) pairs with $\FG \le \alpha$, by policy and $\alpha$. \textbf{Greedy} meets $\alpha = 0.30$, the lower bound of the inequality band and just above the inside-the-ladder anchor of Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description} ($0.28$), on 45--50\% of the days, $0.35$ on nine days in ten and $0.40$ on every day under auto-accept; \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} meets no level of the grid on any day. No other first-generation configuration meets $\alpha \le 0.40$ on more than 4\% of the days under either regime (\textbf{ILP} under auto-accept); at $0.45$ the best of them (\textbf{ILP}) reaches 18\% of the days under the manual regime and 46\% under auto-accept, and at $0.50$ the proposed policies reach 37--46\%. The lower half of the $\alpha$ grid is therefore within reach only of the dispatcher that spreads the work, and the parameter of each proposed policy moves compliance by a few points at most. The second generation changes the lower half of the grid (last block of the table): with $k_d = 25$, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} meet $\alpha = 0.40$ on 40--49\% of the days under the manual regime and 56--64\% under auto-accept, $0.45$ on 61--63\% and 67--83\%, and $0.50$ on 72--82\% and 81--96\%, and still on fewer than 3\% at $0.30$; with $k_d = 50$, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} meets $0.40$ on 54\% and 73\% of the days, $0.45$ on 72\% and 87\%, and $0.30$ on 2\% and 26\%, at unchanged coverage and $1.3$ times the CPU time of $k_d = 25$ ($6.4$ and $5.5$ minutes per day). We therefore take $\alpha^* = 0.40$, the upper bound of the inequality band, as the operating point: not a value derived from the couriers' or a regulator's perspective, but the tightest level of the band that an order-centric policy meets on most days under both regimes at no coverage cost (Table~\ref{tab:alpha}). It is a target met on 54--73\% of the days, not a daily guarantee; $0.45$ is the level that every second-generation variant with $k_d \ge 25$ meets on most days.

Figures~\ref{fig:pareto} and \ref{fig:pareto_late} (the latter in \ref{sec:App_metrics}) show the frontiers traced by the three policies in their own parameter: $\alpha$ for \textbf{MIP-Fair}, $\gamma$ for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ for \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}; each point is a configuration averaged over the 24 instances and the seeds available, and the benchmarks of X1 appear as single points. The three frontiers occupy a narrow band on the right of each panel, between $0.53$ and $0.55$, and \textbf{Greedy} stands alone on the left at $0.30$: no configuration of the three policies trades service for equity along its own parameter at a scale comparable to the gap between the two groups. The sweep-by-sweep reading is in \ref{sec:App_metrics}.

\label{sec:results_history}

One of our main modelling contributions is the equity history in the state. In this section, we analyse how the Gini coefficient evolves within the day under the policies that read $\Hist_t$ and under the policy that does not. Figure~\ref{fig:intraday} shows the trajectory of the Gini coefficient of hourly earnings so far, computed over the eligible couriers connected so far, idle ones at zero, for \textbf{Greedy}, \textbf{Hierarchical}, \textbf{MIP-Fair} ($\alpha = 0.30$) and the second-generation \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} of Section~\ref{sec:results_quality} ($\gamma = 40$, deficit-aware prefilter with $k_d = 25$) on one Bogot\'a day (instance 0, manual regime, seed 1: 10{,}215 orders, 2{,}650 couriers in the zone, of whom 2{,}560 are eligible). The policies separate between 10:00 and 16:00, the hours in which most of the fleet connects. \textbf{Greedy} puts the new couriers to work as they arrive and ends the day at $0.23$. \textbf{Hierarchical} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} keep assigning to the couriers already in motion and end the day at $0.48$ and $0.47$ with 15--16\% of the eligible couriers idle, although on the active couriers (right panel) both stay below $\alpha$ until 16:30 and 19:50: the statistic they read reports compliance during the hours in which the fleet grows. \subsection{Decision making}
\label{sec:results_decisions}

We next analyse how the three policies change the dispatching decision compared to \textbf{Greedy}: who receives which route, and what it pays; the full analysis is given next to the distribution it discusses (Figure~\ref{fig:violin} in \ref{sec:App_population}). The statutory minimum $w^* = 4{,}368$ COP/h lies above the 99th percentile of the end-of-day hourly earnings under every policy, which is the content of $\FD \ge 0.97$. The policies do not compress the distribution of \textbf{Greedy}; they stretch it at both ends: \textbf{Greedy} leaves 3\% of the eligible couriers idle and the six order-centric policies 19--25\%, with a higher third quartile, because the orders that \textbf{Greedy} spreads over the couriers who happen to be free go to the couriers who are best placed, who are then best placed again. Bundling plays no role in these differences: the mean bundle size is $1.03$ orders per route under \textbf{Greedy} and the BRKGA-type policies, $1.01$ under \textbf{MIP-Fair} and $1.00$ under the \textbf{ILP} and \textbf{Hierarchical} policies. For \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} the pay goes where acceptance is at stake: the multiplier raises the pay of the long routes that would otherwise be rejected and lowers that of the short ones that would not, so the bill falls while the pay per kilometre of what is accepted stays above the threshold.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis across operational settings}
\label{sec:results_sensitivity}

Finally, we study how different operational conditions affect the performance and relative advantages of the policies. We vary one factor at a time around the base case (manual regime, $\theta = 903$ COP/km, the $\alpha$ grid) and report, for the benchmark dispatchers and the proposed policies at their selected configurations, the relative difference over the base-case value of the policy, following the one-factor-at-a-time design. Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reports the results for coverage; the regime and $\alpha$ columns come from X1 (from X3 for the rows with $k_d$), the $\theta$ columns from X2, which re-runs six configurations at the ends of the 95\% interval of the logit. Every cell is relative to the same configuration's base-case value on the seeds of its experiment (ten in X1, five in X2 and X3), and a dash marks a parameter the policy does not have or a level not run in X2 or X3. Circuity is not in the table: in the pilot, \textbf{Greedy} covered 6\% more at circuity 1.0 than at 1.3.

\paragraph{Acceptance regime} Figure~\ref{fig:regime_loss} in \ref{sec:App_sens_gini} shows the regime loss, manual minus auto-accept, paired by instance. In coverage it is between $1.6$ points (\textbf{BRKGA-Fair}) and $4.9$ (\textbf{ILP}), with every interval excluding zero; the policies that re-optimise the round after a rejection lose least, and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} loses $1.6$ points more than \textbf{BRKGA} because its pay cut lowers the acceptance of long routes. The regime moves the end-of-day Gini coefficient by at most $0.02$, so the ranking of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} holds under both regimes. With a flat tariff a 4-km single order is accepted with probability 0.19, so coverage under manual acceptance is bounded by behaviour, and auto-accept removes that bound by moving the courier's decision to connection time: the regime matters more than the policy for coverage, and the policy more than the regime for equity.

\paragraph{Behavioural threshold} The threshold $\theta$ is a grade-C parameter estimated from 32 decisions, so X2 re-runs the six configurations of Section~\ref{sec:design} at the ends of its 95\% interval, $\theta = 615$ and $1{,}242$ COP/km, paired with the same seeds at $\theta = 903$ (three \textbf{MIP-Fair} runs timed out at $1{,}242$ and their pairs are dropped). With the flat tariff a single order is accepted with probability one half at $3.6$, $2.4$ and $1.8$ km for the three thresholds, so the response of coverage is asymmetric: $+2.0$ to $+3.7\%$ at $615$ and $-5.6$ to $-9.1\%$ at $1{,}242$. \textbf{Greedy}, which does not re-optimise after a rejection, loses most and falls from fourth to last at the upper end, where \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} overtakes it. The end-of-day Gini coefficient moves by at most $6.8\%$ (no difference larger than $0.027$, for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with $k_d = 50$) and its ranking is the same at the three thresholds; compliance of the second generation of X2 ($k_d = 25$, paired runs) with $\alpha = 0.40$ moves from 38--49\% at $\theta = 903$ to 46--51\% at $615$ and 39--40\% at $1{,}242$, and with $0.45$ from 60--63\% to 52\% at the upper end. The operating point behind $\alpha^* = 0.40$, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} ($\gamma = 40$, $k_d = 50$), meets $\alpha = 0.40$ on 54\% of the days at $\theta = 903$, 68\% at $615$ and 50\% at $1{,}242$ (with $0.45$: 72, 76 and 62\%), while its coverage moves by $+2.1\%$ and $-6.5\%$ and its mean lateness by $-0.30$ and $+0.28$ min.

\paragraph{Tolerated inequality and the choice of $\alpha$} Three complementary criteria are used to choose $\alpha$ (Table~\ref{tab:alpha} in \ref{sec:App_sens_gini}): A, the courier-centric anchor, the end-of-day Gini coefficient of \textbf{Greedy}, $0.30$ on the ten seeds of X1 ($0.27$--$0.32$ by city); B, the inequality band, whose conventional bounds read a Gini coefficient below about 0.30 as low and above about 0.40 as high, placed with the survey anchors of 0.28 inside the bonus ladder and 0.41 outside it (\ref{sec:App_sens_gini}), so $\alpha = 0.30$; and C, the Pareto knee, the point of maximum distance from the chord between the extremes of the coverage--Gini frontier. Criteria A and B coincide at $0.30$ on these data, because \textbf{Greedy} is itself inside the low-inequality band, and among the policies of Table~\ref{tab:compliance} only \textbf{Greedy} meets it on more than 3\% of the days (45--50\%), except the $k_d = 50$ variants under auto-accept (\textbf{BRKGA-Fair} 26\%, \textbf{MIP-Fair} 4\%). Criterion C does not apply: coverage does not fall along either sweep, so there is no knee to find. The interval is therefore the band itself, $[0.30, 0.40]$: $0.30$ is the courier-centric anchor, which the order-centric policies with $k_d \le 25$ reach on fewer than 3\% of the days and \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} on none; $0.40$, the upper bound of the band, is met by \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with $k_d = 50$ on 54\% of the days under the manual regime and 73\% under auto-accept at no coverage cost; and $0.45$, above the band, is the smallest value that every second-generation variant with $k_d \ge 25$ meets on most days under both regimes. The recommendation is therefore conditional: a platform that values the coverage and lateness gain of the order-centric policies can operate at $\alpha^* = 0.40$ with \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} ($\gamma = 40$, $k_d = 50$), which costs $5.5$--$6.4$ minutes of CPU per day against $22$--$51$ for \textbf{MIP-Fair} with $k_d = 50$ at a lower compliance (50--54\%), and accepts that the target is missed on a quarter to a half of the days; a platform that holds to $\alpha \le 0.35$ should use the courier-centric rule with the least-served-first priority, which meets it on nine days in ten, whereas in order of log-in the same rule meets it on none.

\paragraph{Supply, the benchmark's priority and the candidate set} Three further experiments (1{,}680 runs, \ref{sec:App_review}) test the setting behind the central result. With the fleet thinned to one courier in three, a sensitivity scenario that brings the zones to about the orders per connected hour that surveyed couriers report inside the bonus ladder (Section~\ref{sec:instances}), coverage falls by $2.7$--$5.0$ points, 96--100\% of the eligible couriers are active, and the end-of-day Gini coefficient is $0.21$ for \textbf{Greedy}, $0.24$--$0.25$ for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and $0.21$--$0.22$ for its second generation: the size of the gap depends on how far couriers outnumber the work. \textbf{Greedy-Arrival} (Table~\ref{tab:review_arrival}) confirms that the equity of \textbf{Greedy} comes entirely from its least-served-first priority (Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}). The \textbf{ILP} on the eight candidates of \textbf{MIP-Fair} covers $3.8$ points less than \textbf{MIP-Fair} at $\alpha = 1$ under manual acceptance and ends within $0.008$ of it in Gini: the advantage of \textbf{MIP-Fair} is its tie-breaking stage, and its Gini constraint is inert.

\paragraph{Fleet composition and emissions} Fleet composition sets the emissions level, from $0.13$--$0.18$ kg CO$_2$e per delivered order in Bogot\'a to $0.25$--$0.36$ in Pereira, and the policy sets the rest in the opposite direction to equity: \textbf{Greedy} drives 30\% more per delivered order than the order-centric policies because it sends the least-served courier rather than the nearest one, and emits $0.280$ kg per order against $0.213$--$0.250$ (Table~\ref{tab:emissions} in \ref{sec:App_sens_gini}). In summary, the regime bounds coverage, the policy sets equity where couriers outnumber work, the threshold does not reorder the policies within its interval, and the fleet composition sets the emissions level.

\section{Findings and Discussion}
\label{sec:findings_discussion}



We begin with the first research question, whether an end-of-day guarantee requires the equity history in the state. The results show that fairness enforced within each window does not deliver fairness at the end of the day: the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy balances the pay offered among the free couriers of every round and ends the day with a Gini coefficient of $0.53$ over the eligible couriers against $0.30$ for \textbf{Greedy}, because the per-window statistic is computed on the couriers of the round and ignores both what each has already earned and who was left out of the round. Reading $\Hist_t$ is necessary but not sufficient: the three policies that read it ended the day as unequal as the order-centric policies that do not, because they read a productivity rate over the couriers of the round. The content of the history must be exposure, earnings per hour connected, and its population the eligible couriers, so that a courier with little work over a long connection is read as under-served rather than as productive; and the idle couriers, whose deficit is one under either reading, must reach the solver through the candidate set, which is the signal that the courier-centric dispatcher obtains by serving the least-served courier first; \textbf{Greedy-Arrival}, which serves the couriers in order of log-in, ends at $0.75$, so the benchmark that meets the guarantee also needs the history. The second generation of X3 confirms the diagnosis: with exposure as the content and the deficit-aware prefilter as the way to reach the idle couriers, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} close 42--56\% of the gap with $k_d = 25$ and \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} 56--77\% with $k_d = 50$, at no coverage cost. \textbf{Greedy} is not tuned to the statistic on which it wins: it ranks couriers by orders delivered, not by earnings per connected hour, and does not know who will be eligible (\ref{sec:App_objections}).

We next address the second research question concerning which lever satisfies the guarantee at the lowest cost. On the ten seeds of X1 the answer is the assignment rule itself: the courier-centric rule with the least-served-first priority meets $\alpha = 0.40$ on every day under auto-accept and on 98\% of the days under the manual regime at a cost, under manual acceptance, of $1.2$ points of coverage against the best order-centric policy and of $0.5$--$1.8$ minutes of lateness against the six order-centric policies ($0.2$--$1.1$ under auto-accept), while no order-centric policy of X1 meets it on more than 4\% of the days. Among the three proposed policies, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} is the only one whose parameter moves the end-of-day statistic in the right direction at no coverage cost; the $\alpha$ of \textbf{MIP-Fair} is inert because, with coverage first and only $k = 8$ candidates per route, the coverage-optimal assignments of a round leave the Gini block almost nothing to choose; and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} is the only policy that moves pay, which lowers the platform's bill by 12\% (manual) to 20\% (auto-accept) of the tariff but does not reach the couriers who are left idle. The second generation of X3 narrows but does not close this difference: reading exposure instead of activity and offering the most-deprived free couriers to the solver halves the gap to \textbf{Greedy} ($\FG = 0.44$ manual, $0.40$--$0.42$ auto-accept) at no coverage cost and at most $0.3$ minutes of lateness, so a second-generation \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} meets $\alpha = 0.45$ on 63--67\% of the days, and $\alpha^* = 0.40$ on 54--73\% with $k_d = 50$, while keeping the point of coverage that \textbf{Greedy} gives up. Part of the remaining gap is a matter of candidates and part is structural: with $k_d = 50$ the heuristic reaches $0.40$ (manual) and $0.35$ (auto-accept), whereas the exact round model flattens, because an order-centric round assigns at most one route per courier and the coverage objective fixes which couriers it needs. Because coverage is flat across policies at the scale of a dispatching zone, the cost of the guarantee is paid mainly in lateness and in who receives the routes. Dispatching alone cannot lift couriers to the statutory minimum wage at the current tariff; what it can do is distribute the shortfall evenly, which is the practical content of using $\FG$ as the constraint and $\FD$ as a diagnostic.

Finally, we address the third research question concerning the operational conditions under which the guarantee is affordable. The first condition is supply: with the fleet thinned to one courier in three, \textbf{Greedy} and the two \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} variants run on it end the day at a mean Gini coefficient of $0.21$--$0.25$, below $\alpha = 0.30$ (Table~\ref{tab:review_thin}), so the choice of dispatcher matters where couriers outnumber work, as they do in the connection logs and, outside the bonus ladder, in the survey. The acceptance regime matters more than the policy in the manual world, where coverage is bounded by behaviour; the sanctions that moved the workforce to auto-accept are the platform's way of removing that bound, and our results quantify what the bound costs. Within the confidence interval of the behavioural threshold the fairness ranking of the policies does not change, and the coverage ranking changes only at the upper end, where \textbf{Greedy}, which does not re-optimise after a rejection, loses most (9\% of its coverage against 5.6--7.3\% for the policies that do). The three criteria for $\alpha$ give an interval rather than a point, the band $[0.30, 0.40]$; the order-centric policies reach its upper bound on most days under both regimes only with the widest candidate set ($k_d = 50$; $k_d = 25$ suffices under auto-accept), so the choice between them at $\alpha^* = 0.40$ and the least-served-first rule at $0.35$ is a managerial trade-off of a point of coverage and about a minute of lateness (1.0 manual, 0.7 auto-accept) against five to ten points of Gini. The pay lever and the assignment lever are complements: the first buys acceptance where couriers reject, the second distributes work where they do not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:Conclusion}

In this paper, we study a dispatching problem in which a platform repeatedly offers work and pay to self-employed agents who may reject it, and in which the platform is accountable for the distribution of the agents' earnings at the end of the day. We examine this structure in meal delivery, where we formulate the problem as a constrained Markov decision process with an equity history in the state, an end-of-day Gini constraint on hourly earnings over the couriers connected for at least one hour, idle ones included, and acceptance that responds to pay per kilometre in one of two regimes. As a methodological contribution, we develop three cost function approximations that read the equity history in every round, including a linearisation of the Gini constraint that is exact by blocks of courier pairs, and we evaluate them against five benchmarks on 24 real operating days from six Colombian cities with field-calibrated behaviour. Over the eligible couriers the courier-centric dispatcher that serves the least-served courier first (\textbf{Greedy}) ends the day at a Gini coefficient of $0.30$ and gives work to 97\% of them, while every order-centric policy, including the three proposed, ends between $0.52$ and $0.56$ under manual acceptance ($0.50$--$0.57$ under auto-accept) by leaving a quarter of them idle, a gap that falls to $0.03$--$0.04$ for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} when the fleet is thinned to one courier in three. Reading exposure over the eligible couriers and offering the most-deprived couriers to the solver closes 42--56\% of that gap with 25 extra candidates per route and, for \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, 56--77\% with 50, at no coverage cost. From a managerial perspective, the central implication is that a fairness guarantee is a property of the day: it should be measured on hourly earnings over a population the dispatcher cannot shrink, enforced through what every courier has earned per hour connected so far, and complemented by the pay lever where couriers reject.

Beyond meal delivery, the equity history and the end-of-day constraint apply to any sequential assignment problem in which the agents' outcomes accumulate over a horizon and the regulator or the platform cares about their distribution at its end, such as ride-hailing, crowdsourced delivery and task platforms. Within this architecture, the fairness metric, the acceptance model and the per-round problem can each be replaced; the block linearisation applies to any constraint in mean absolute differences in which each agent's outcome takes finitely many values per round.

The study also has several limitations related to the decision environment. The behavioural evidence is from Bogot\'a, and its use in the other five cities is a transfer assumption; the five cities are robustness checks, not independent calibrations. The acceptance logit rests on 32 decisions by 11 interviewees, with wide intervals, and reflects the 2020 manual regime; we report the sensitivity over the interval of the threshold and run both regimes, but the slope interval was not swept and the parameters are grade C. Validating the logit against the platform's offer logs, unavailable to us, and modelling the repositioning of idle couriers are future work. The connection logs give 0.36--0.40 orders per connected courier-hour, close to the 0.35 that surveyed couriers report outside the bonus ladder and a third of the 1.15 they report inside it; the survey estimates the share of time inside the ladder only indirectly (about a quarter, implied by declared monthly income; in the zones, 0.9\% of the connected courier-hours fall in clock hours with at least the in-ladder 1.15 orders per courier-hour, against 43\% with the thinned fleet, \ref{sec:App_objections}), so the supply level is uncertain, and with the fleet thinned to one courier in three the gap between \textbf{Greedy} and \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} falls to $0.03$--$0.04$ (Section~\ref{sec:results_sensitivity}); a calibration of supply against platform-side activity logs is future work. The 24 instances are four days from each of six cities, which the standard errors treat as exchangeable; a city-blocked bootstrap would be more conservative. The survey reports pay in bands, and 39 of the 42 interviewees who discussed it (93\%) report that the pay shown changed after acceptance (median realised-to-shown ratio 0.60 over 17 cases); the model responds to the pay shown, so realised earnings may be lower. The equity history is intraday whereas the platform's bonus ladder is a multi-day mechanism, and the model has no sanctions, no acceptance-rate dynamics, no cash-handling constraint and no heterogeneity by seniority or nationality. Circuity, vehicle speeds, the 30-minute slack and the UK emission factors are declared assumptions without local sources. Cross-zone effects are not modelled. Future research could extend the history to several days, add the dynamics of sanctions and acceptance rates, and calibrate the behavioural model in a second city.

There are also methodological limitations that motivate further research. \textbf{MIP-Fair} uses a candidate prefilter of eight couriers per route for budget reasons and a 10-second limit per stage; a column-generation or a rolling subset of pairs could relax both. Whether a per-round model over all the free couriers closes the rest of the gap is open; with $k_d = 50$ \textbf{MIP-Fair} already flattens at $0.40$--$0.41$. The three policies are tuned by enumeration over a grid; learning the deficit weight or the pay multiplier as a function of the state, as in \cite{stein2024learning}, could reduce the cost of the guarantee further, at the price of an offline training effort. The minimum-wage constraint is not imposed because it is infeasible at the current tariff; a joint design of tariff and dispatching, in which the platform's budget is a decision, is a natural extension. Finally, the 240 public instances of \cite{reyes2018meal} could be run with the policies already in the simulator to test the findings outside Colombia.
