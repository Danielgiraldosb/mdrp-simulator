\begin{abstract}
We study dispatching problems in which a platform repeatedly assigns work to self-employed agents who may reject it, and in which the platform is accountable for how the earnings of those agents are distributed at the end of the day rather than within each decision window. Our focal application is meal delivery, where every two minutes a platform offers routes and pay to crowdsourced couriers, couriers accept or reject according to what the offer pays per kilometre, and fairness-aware dispatching has so far balanced work within the window while assuming that offers are always accepted. We formulate the problem as a constrained Markov decision process whose state carries an equity history (each courier's accumulated earnings, orders and active time), whose constraint bounds the end-of-day Gini coefficient of hourly earnings by a threshold $\alpha$, and whose transition draws acceptance from a logit on pay per kilometre with two empirically observed regimes, manual acceptance and auto-accept. The formulation contains corporate, workload-balance and minimum-wage dispatching as special cases. We develop three policies that solve the same problem inside one simulator against four benchmarks: \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} biases a genetic decoder by the equity deficit, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} also sets per-offer pay within a bounded band, and \textbf{MIP-Fair} solves a per-window mixed-integer programme with a block-exact linearisation of the Gini constraint. Acceptance is calibrated from 135 intercept surveys and 44 interviews with couriers in Bogot\'a. Using 24 operating days from six Colombian cities, we quantify the coverage and punctuality given up for the guarantee by policy, by acceptance regime and by threshold, and select $\alpha$ by three criteria. \todo{Two sentences with the headline numbers from X1: cost of the guarantee at $\alpha^*$ and the regime loss.} Preliminary runs show that fairness enforced window by window increases end-of-day inequality, and that the pay lever retains coverage with 8--18\% less total pay.

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Dispatching problems arise when a limited set of agents must be matched repeatedly to requests that appear over time. In a corporate fleet, the agents are vehicles: an assignment is executed as soon as it is made, and the quality of a dispatching policy is measured by the service it delivers to customers \citep{Dantzig1959,pillac2013review}. In platform operations, however, the agents are self-employed workers who decide whether to execute each assignment and who earn their living from the sum of the assignments they execute over a day. Two features of such settings are absent from the corporate model. First, an assignment is an offer: whether it is accepted depends on what it pays relative to the effort it asks for, so the transition of the system is endogenous to the pay decision. Second, the platform is accountable for a distributional outcome, the earnings of its workers at the end of the day, which no single decision window determines but every decision window affects.

Meal delivery is the focus of this work and the setting in which these features are sharpest. On-demand platforms coordinate restaurants, customers and a crowd of couriers through an assignment decision repeated every few minutes. The operational literature on the meal delivery routing problem (MDRP) has made that decision efficient: rolling-horizon re-optimisation, order bundling and anticipatory assignment deliver high coverage with short click-to-door times \citep{reyes2018meal,yildiz2019provably,Ulmer2021}. The couriers' side of the problem has received less attention. A courier earns over a day, not over a two-minute window, so a dispatcher that balances work within each window can still produce a very unequal distribution of daily hourly earnings when couriers connect at different times or when some of them systematically receive the better routes. Moreover, a courier is not a vehicle: in the field, couriers reason in pay per kilometre and reject long, low-paying routes, and platforms have moved their workforce to an auto-accept regime, backed by sanctions, precisely because manual rejection erodes coverage. Similar structures arise in ride-hailing, where income inequality among drivers has been traded against efficiency \citep{lesmana2019balancing}, in crowdsourced last-mile delivery \citep{sobhanan2024equity} and in gig platforms that guarantee an hourly wage by paying for idle time \citep{nair2022work4food}. These settings differ in detail, but they share a platform that decides who receives which work and at what pay, workers who respond to that pay, and a fairness outcome that is defined over a horizon longer than the decision window.

Methodologically, these problems are challenging because the fairness guarantee, the acceptance behaviour and the routing decision are coupled within the same sequential process. The value of offering a route to a courier depends on whether the courier will accept it, which depends on the pay; the pay changes the platform's cost and the courier's earnings; and the earnings accumulated by every courier determine whether the end-of-day guarantee can still be met by the remaining windows of the day. Anticipation is therefore required, but of a particular kind: not of future demand, which rolling-horizon dispatching handles procedurally, but of the distributional consequence of the current assignment at the end of the day. In the meal-delivery setting, this means jointly deciding, window by window, who receives each route and what the offer pays, while keeping track of what every courier has earned and worked so far.

We address this problem as a constrained Markov decision process (CMDP, \citealt{altman1999constrained}). The state carries, in addition to the positions of couriers and the pool of orders, an \emph{equity history} $\Hist_t$ with the earnings, orders and active time accumulated by every courier. The constraint bounds the end-of-day Gini coefficient of hourly earnings by a tolerated inequality $\alpha$. The transition draws the acceptance of every offer from a logit on pay per kilometre, estimated from field data, with a manual regime in which couriers reject and an auto-accept regime in which they do not. We then develop three policies that read $\Hist_t$ when deciding the current window. \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} biases the decoder of a biased random-key genetic algorithm by each courier's equity deficit; \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} extends the chromosome with a pay gene per courier and sets the offer within a bounded band around the tariff; and \textbf{MIP-Fair} solves a per-window mixed-integer programme in which the Gini constraint is written on the projected end-of-window history and linearised exactly by blocks of courier pairs. In terms of the framework of \cite{powell2021reinforcement}, all three are cost function approximations: they modify the optimisation problem solved in each window with terms computed from $\Hist_t$ instead of learning a value of the future. The three policies share route candidates, decision window, acceptance model and metrics with four benchmarks inside a single discrete-event simulator, so that the frontiers they trace in $\alpha$, in the deficit weight and in the pay weight are comparable.

This study is guided by three research questions that connect the structure of the decision problem, the proposed policies, and the operational conditions under which the guarantee is affordable. First, does an end-of-day fairness guarantee require the equity history in the state, or can fairness enforced within each window deliver it? Second, which lever satisfies the guarantee at the lowest cost in coverage and punctuality, choosing who receives each route or choosing what the offer pays, and does the answer depend on the acceptance regime? Third, under which operational conditions, acceptance regime, logit threshold, tolerated inequality and fleet composition, is the guarantee cheap or expensive, and which $\alpha$ is sustainable for a platform? The paper makes three contributions. First, as a theoretical and modelling contribution, we formulate meal-delivery dispatching as a CMDP with an intertemporal equity history in the state, an end-of-day Gini constraint and price-endogenous acceptance. The formulation contains the corporate model ($\alpha = 1$), workload-balance models and minimum-wage models as special cases, and we show by a numerical example why per-window fairness does not imply end-of-day fairness. Second, as a methodological contribution, we develop three cost function approximations for the same CMDP, including a block-exact linearisation of the Gini constraint that reduces the per-window programme from $O(n^2)$ pairs to a problem solvable in a fraction of a second with thousands of connected couriers, and we verify all three against exhaustive enumeration on a toy instance. Third, as a managerial contribution, we measure the cost of the fairness guarantee on real operations: 24 days from six cities with field-calibrated behaviour, Pareto frontiers per policy and regime, the loss attributable to the acceptance regime, the sensitivity to the behavioural threshold, the choice of $\alpha$ by three criteria, and the side effect on emissions through fleet composition. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first MDRP formulation in which fairness is guaranteed at the end of the day rather than per window and in which courier acceptance responds to the pay the platform decides.

\paragraph{Problem-related literature}

The MDRP was defined by \cite{reyes2018meal} as a rolling-horizon, dynamic-deterministic problem: at each re-optimisation epoch a deterministic instance is solved and the horizon rolls forward as new orders arrive, so uncertainty is handled procedurally rather than through anticipation. Their public instance library (Grubhub) has become the standard benchmark. \cite{yildiz2019provably} obtained provably high-quality solutions for a deterministic variant and found that bundling by itself does not reduce travel much; courier capacity and shift scheduling are the main levers. \cite{steever2019dynamic} revise assignments whenever an order arrives or traffic changes; \cite{Ulmer2021} delay assignment until preparation-time uncertainty resolves and anticipate it with approximate dynamic programming; \cite{liu2021multiperiod} dispatch and route on-demand deliveries from stores with random demand. Later contributions address shared logistics \citep{bi2023meal}, courier satisfaction through dynamic operating regions \citep{auad2023courier}, order batching for crowdsourced fleets \citep{simoni2023crowdsourced} and ghost kitchens \citep{neria2024restaurant}. The degree-of-dynamism criterion of \cite{pillac2013review} justifies a rolling horizon for a problem in which essentially all orders are revealed after the day starts; for the broader class of stochastic dynamic vehicle routing problems we refer to \cite{SOEFFKER2022801} and \cite{hildebrandt2022opportunities}, and for the modelling of such problems as sequential decision processes to \cite{ulmer2020modeling}. Across these models, acceptance is perfect or exogenous and couriers are treated as vehicles. This literature motivates our corporate benchmarks: the \textbf{Greedy} dispatcher reproduces the platform's practice of assigning each order to the nearest feasible courier, and the \textbf{ILP} is the exact per-window coverage dispatcher that our \textbf{MIP-Fair} policy extends.

Fairness has entered delivery and ride-hailing platforms mostly as \emph{balance}. \cite{wen2010dynamic} balance workload and income opportunities within a multi-period routing model, and \cite{liao2020multi} balance workload in meal delivery with a two-stage NSGA-II, arguing that an equitable distribution is itself an incentive. \cite{lesmana2019balancing} define fairness as income inequality among ride-sourcing drivers and trace the efficiency--fairness frontier with a reassignment algorithm applied batch by batch. FairFoody \citep{gupta2022fairfoody} and WORK4FOOD \citep{nair2022work4food} treat fairness as income distribution among couriers in Indian food-delivery data: the former balances earnings by assignment, the latter guarantees a minimum hourly wage by controlling the number of active couriers and paying for idle time. \cite{martinez2024exploring} explore fairness constraints in food-delivery routing and scheduling on UK data and find moderate efficiency costs, and \cite{sobhanan2024equity} give bi-objective equity--cost frontiers for crowdsourced last-mile delivery. These works share two limits that this paper removes: fairness is enforced per window or per instance, or through a reward term whose end-of-day consequence is not guaranteed, and couriers accept whatever is assigned. The per-window logic motivates our \textbf{Hierarchical} benchmark, which equalises earnings among the couriers of each window and ignores the history; comparing it with \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} isolates the value of carrying $\Hist_t$ in the state. On the economic side, \cite{reyes2018meal} and \cite{yildiz2019provably} include a shift-level guaranteed minimum set by the company, \cite{bajec2022strategic} use Shapley-value incentives for sustainable crowdshipping, and the algorithmic-fairness literature shows that short- and long-horizon fairness can diverge \citep{corbettdavies2023measure}. None of these integrates pay as a dispatching decision to which acceptance responds.

Courier behaviour has been modelled mainly as availability. Reinforcement-learning approaches \citep{ahamed2020deep,bozanta2022courier} include courier availability in the state but keep acceptance exogenous, and \cite{haferkamp2023heatmap} address repositioning. The closest precedent to endogenous acceptance is the discrete-event framework of \cite{quintero2020computational}, in which the probability of accepting a notification depends on the assignment distance and a courier-level parameter, but not on the pay offered. Customer acceptance has been anticipated in delivery routing \citep{ULMER2020183}, but there it is the platform that accepts requests, not the agent who accepts work. No MDRP study models acceptance as a function of the offered pay, although a courier offered a long, low-paying route is more likely to reject it, which biases both coverage and fairness estimates. \todo{One sentence on what \citet{rl2025fairdelivery} (arXiv:2501.05808) actually does, after reading it.}

\paragraph{Methodological literature}

Our policies belong to the class of cost function approximations in the framework of \cite{powell2021reinforcement} and \cite{POWELL2019795}: the problem solved in each window is modified by terms, the equity deficit, the projected Gini excess and the pay excess, that encode the end-of-day consequence of the current decision, without learning a value function. This is the same class as rule-based and parametrised policies in the dynamic routing literature \citep{SOEFFKER2022801}, where parameters are either tuned by enumeration or learned by simulation \citep{stein2024learning}. We tune the deficit weight, the tolerated inequality and the pay weight by enumeration over a grid and report the full frontier, since the trade-off itself is the object of study. Two methodological components are specific to this paper. The first is the biased random-key genetic algorithm \citep{goncalves2011biased} with rolling horizon that we ported from our earlier work on the MDRP \citep{giraldo2026brkga,giraldo2024grasp}; the decoder is the component that reads $\Hist_t$. The second is the linearisation of the Gini constraint, which in its natural form requires one variable per pair of couriers; we show that, because a courier takes at most one route per window, only the pairs of free couriers need variables and the remaining pairs enter as constants or as linear terms, and we verify the reformulation against the all-pairs model. The Gini coefficient is used in its mean-absolute-difference form \citep{yitzhaki1979relative}. To the best of our knowledge, the proposed policies are the first to control an end-of-day inequality constraint on real meal-delivery operations with acceptance that responds to the pay the platform decides.

\section{Problem definition}
\label{sec:problem_definition}

In this section, we define the fair meal-delivery dispatching problem and formulate it as a constrained sequential decision process. We first describe the problem and the field evidence on courier behaviour that shapes it. We then define the components of the process. An illustrative numerical example connecting the formulation to the dispatching decisions, and showing why per-window fairness does not imply end-of-day fairness, is provided in \ref{sec:example}.

\subsection{Problem description}
\label{sec:Problem_description}

A platform connects a set of stores $R$, a set of users $U$ and a set of couriers $C$ over one operating day. Stores and couriers (connection time, position and vehicle) are known in advance; orders are revealed at placement time. Every two minutes, the dispatcher assigns to each available courier at most one route, where a route collects up to $Q$ orders from a single store and delivers them to their users, and offers it with a pay. The courier accepts or rejects the offer. Accepted routes are executed (pick-up, drop-off); the orders of rejected routes return to the pool for the next window. Couriers are notified only while idle or picking up. An order is cancelled, and counted against coverage, when no feasible assignment exists before its hard service deadline. We assume that each user places at most one order per day and that a cancelled order does not re-enter the system, as in the public MDRP instances \citep{reyes2018meal}.

Three aspects distinguish this problem from the corporate MDRP, and each is supported by field evidence collected with couriers of the platform in Bogot\'a (44 semi-structured interviews in 2020--2021 and an intercept survey of 135 couriers in May--June 2021; the full assumption-versus-evidence table is in the online supplement). First, \emph{acceptance responds to pay per kilometre}. Couriers reason in pay per kilometre (\enquote{nobody takes 4.1 km for 3{,}300}); in the survey, pay (importance 5.71 on a 7-point scale) and distance (5.65) are the dominant factors in that order. A logit on $\ln(\text{pay/km})$ fitted to 32 acceptance decisions by 11 interviewees gives an indifference threshold of 903 COP/km (95\% interval [615; 1{,}242]) and slope 2.89 ([2.02; 5.23]). Second, \emph{there are two decision regimes}. In the 2020 interviews no courier auto-accepted (0 of 12) and rejection was common (13 of 14); by 2021, 23 of 25 auto-accepted and 2 of 12 rejected. In the survey 64\% auto-accept always or almost always; behind the shift are platform sanctions for rejecting (60\% of surveyed couriers had been blocked, median 24 hours), and auto-accepting protects against them (odds ratio 2.76). In the auto-accept regime the courier's decision is whether to be connected at all, which the data record as on and off times. Third, \emph{the tariff is flat in distance}. The per-kilometre rate estimated from the interviews is 155 COP/km with a 95\% interval [$-103$; 413] and $R^2 = 0.02$; the survey reports 2{,}200 COP per order outside the platform's bonus ladder. We therefore model a flat tariff and treat per-offer pay as a policy instrument, not as a description of current practice.

The evidence also fixes the fairness outcome. Median hourly earnings while connected were 2{,}622 COP in the survey and 3{,}957 COP in the interviews; 91.9\% of surveyed couriers earned less than the 2021 statutory minimum wage per hour (4{,}368 COP), and the platform guarantees no hourly pay. The Gini coefficient of daily income among surveyed couriers is 0.39 outside the bonus ladder and 0.26 inside it. We therefore measure fairness on hourly earnings at the end of the day, normalised by active time so that connecting late is not penalised, and we use the two survey values as anchors for the tolerated inequality. Service and decision times are taken from the same sources: a median in-store wait of 20 minutes with a purchased order (60 minutes in the stressed interview accounts) and a median decision time of 16 seconds for an offer displayed for 20 seconds. We note that the bonus ladder is a multi-day mechanism whereas our equity history is intraday, and that all behavioural evidence is from Bogot\'a; both are discussed as limitations in Section~\ref{sec:Conclusion}.

The goal of the platform is to find a dispatching and pay-setting policy that minimises expected undelivered orders and lateness over the day while guaranteeing that the end-of-day Gini coefficient of the couriers' hourly earnings does not exceed a tolerated value $\alpha$.

\subsection{Sequential decision process}
\label{sec:SDP}

The problem at hand is stochastic and dynamic. Orders are revealed over the day, preparation times are uncertain, and the acceptance of every offer is a random variable whose distribution depends on the pay the platform decides. Because assignment and pay decisions are made every two minutes and change the earnings and availability of couriers for the rest of the day, we model the problem as a sequential decision process \citep{powell2021reinforcement,ulmer2020modeling,cuellar2023} with a constraint on a terminal quantity, that is, a constrained Markov decision process \citep{altman1999constrained}. In the following, we define its components: decision point and state, decisions, cost function, exogenous information and transition, and the constrained objective.

\paragraph{Decision point and state}

The process consists of decision points $t \in T$ with $T = \{0, 1, \dots, t_{\max}\}$, one at the start of every two-minute window of the day; we refer to the dispatching decision made at $t$ as a \emph{round}. At decision point $t$, the state $S_t$ contains the information accessible to the dispatcher. For our problem, a state contains information on the couriers, the orders waiting to be assigned, their preparation, and what every courier has earned and worked so far. We define the state by the following components:

\begin{compactitem}
    \item the courier information $\mathcal{L}_t$, giving for each courier $c \in C$ its position $\ell_c(t)$, status $\sigma_c(t) \in \{\text{idle, picking-up, in-transit, dropping-off, offline}\}$ and time to next availability $\eta_c(t)$,
    \item the set $\mathcal{O}_t$ of unassigned orders; order $o$ is linked to a store $r_o \in R$ with location $\ell_{r_o}$ and to a user $u_o \in U$ with drop-off location $\ell_{u_o}$, and has placement time $a_o$, ready time $e_o$ and promised drop-off time $d_o$,
    \item the vector $\mathcal{P}_t$ of estimated remaining preparation times $\hat p_o(t)$ of the unassigned orders,
    \item and the equity history $\Hist_t = (h_c(t))_{c \in C}$, with
\end{compactitem}
\begin{equation}
h_c(t) = \bigl(\earn_c(t),\; \ord_c(t),\; \act_c(t)\bigr), \qquad c \in C,
\label{eqn:history}
\end{equation}
the earnings, orders delivered and active (connected) time accumulated by courier $c$ up to $t$. In summary, $S_t = (\mathcal{L}_t, \mathcal{O}_t, \mathcal{P}_t, \Hist_t)$. In the initial state $S_0$ no order has been placed and $h_c(0) = (0, 0, 0)$ for all couriers. The equity history is the component that enables dynamic fairness control: a policy that reads $\Hist_t$ can prevent inequities from compounding across windows. It is absent from existing MDRP formulations, where the state contains the positions and plans of couriers but not what they have earned \citep{ulmer2020modeling}.

Courier $c$ has on-time $e_c$, off-time $l_c > e_c$, initial location $\ell_c$ and vehicle type $v_c \in V$, $V = \{$walking, bicycle, motorcycle, car$\}$. Travel times are deterministic, $\tau(\ell, \ell') = \kappa\, \|\ell - \ell'\| / \nu(v_c)$, with a circuity factor $\kappa$ and a vehicle-dependent speed $\nu(v_c)$; service times $s_r$ at the store and $s_u$ at the user are included in the duration of a route. A route $s$ collects at most $Q$ orders from a single store and is served by a single courier; $\dist(s)$ is its length, $\tau_s$ its duration and $|s|$ the number of orders it contains.

\paragraph{Decisions}

At each decision point $t$, the dispatcher decides which available courier receives which route and what the offer pays. A decision is therefore represented by a set of triples
\begin{equation}
x_t = \bigl\{(c, s, \pi_{c,s}) : c \in C_t^{\text{free}},\; s \in \mathcal{S}_t\bigr\},
\label{eqn:decision}
\end{equation}
where $C_t^{\text{free}}$ is the set of available couriers, $\mathcal{S}_t$ the set of feasible routes that can be built from $\mathcal{O}_t$, and $\pi_{c,s}$ the pay offered for the pair. A route is feasible for a courier if every order can be picked up after its ready time and dropped off before its hard deadline $d_o + \Delta^{\max}$, given the courier's position and speed. A decision $x_t \in \mathcal{X}(S_t)$ is feasible if each courier receives at most one route and each order appears in at most one route.

The pay offer follows the platform's tariff, an affine function of route distance,
\begin{equation}
\pi_{c,s} = \pi_0 \cdot |s| + \pi_1 \cdot \dist(s),
\label{eqn:tariff}
\end{equation}
with $\pi_0$ a base fee per order and $\pi_1$ a per-kilometre rate. Following the field evidence of Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description}, the baseline tariff is flat ($\pi_0 = 2{,}200$ COP, $\pi_1 = 0$). Pay-setting policies may scale the offer by a multiplier $m_c \in [1-\delta, 1+\delta]$ around the tariff; they do not set pay arbitrarily.

\paragraph{Acceptance, exogenous information and transition}

After decision $x_t$ in state $S_t$, two kinds of information are revealed. The first is the acceptance of every offer. Courier $c$ accepts route $s$ offered at pay $\pi_{c,s}$ with probability
\begin{equation}
p_{c,s}(\pi_{c,s}) =
\begin{cases}
\sigma\!\bigl(b\,[\ln(\pi_{c,s}/\dist(s)) - \ln\theta]\bigr) & \text{manual regime},\\[2pt]
1 & \text{auto-accept regime},
\end{cases}
\label{eqn:acceptance}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $\theta$ the pay per kilometre at which acceptance is even and $b$ the slope on log pay per kilometre. Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance} is the form the field evidence supports: couriers evaluate offers by pay per kilometre, and the pay decided by the platform enters the decision directly, which distinguishes it from the distance-only acceptance of \cite{quintero2020computational}. We denote by $\mathbf{b}_t \in \{0,1\}^{|x_t|}$ the vector of Bernoulli acceptance realisations. The second kind of information is exogenous to the decision: $\omega_{t+1} = (\Delta\mathcal{O}_{t+1}, \Delta\mathcal{P}_{t+1}, \Delta\mathcal{A}_{t+1})$ reveals the orders placed during the window, the realised preparation times, and the courier log-ins and log-outs. The transition function $\mathcal{T}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \omega_{t+1})$ leads to the new state $S_{t+1}$ with the following updates:

\begin{compactitem}
    \item accepted routes are executed: the courier's status, position and time to availability in $\mathcal{L}_{t+1}$ follow from the route's pick-up and drop-off sequence, and the orders of accepted routes leave $\mathcal{O}_{t+1}$,
    \item the orders of rejected routes remain in $\mathcal{O}_{t+1}$, together with the new orders $\Delta\mathcal{O}_{t+1}$; orders whose hard deadline can no longer be met are cancelled,
    \item preparation estimates are replaced by realisations where revealed, $\mathcal{P}_{t+1}$,
    \item couriers that log in or out change status according to $\Delta\mathcal{A}_{t+1}$,
    \item and the equity history is updated for every accepted pair: $\earn_c(t+1) = \earn_c(t) + \pay(c, s, \pi_{c,s})$ and $\ord_c(t+1) = \ord_c(t) + |s|$ when the route is completed, while $\act_c(t+1) = \act_c(t) + 2$ minutes for every connected courier, accepted or not.
\end{compactitem}

\paragraph{Cost function}

The cost $\mathcal{C}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \omega_{t+1})$ of a decision penalises orders that are cancelled during the window and the lateness of orders delivered during the window,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \omega_{t+1}) = w_1 \sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}_t} \mathbb{I}[o \text{ cancelled}] + w_2 \sum_{o \text{ delivered}} \max\bigl(0,\; \tau_{\text{drop}}(o) - d_o\bigr),
\label{eqn:cost}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function and $w_1, w_2 > 0$ weigh coverage against punctuality. Its extension with a payment term, used by the pay-setting policy, is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{+}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \omega_{t+1}) = \mathcal{C}(S_t, x_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \omega_{t+1}) + \lambda \sum_{(c,s) \in x_t} b_{c,s}\, \pay(c, s, \pi_{c,s}),
\label{eqn:cost_plus}
\end{equation}
where $\pay(c,s,\pi_{c,s})$ is the amount paid for an accepted route and $\lambda > 0$ trades platform cost against coverage. In our experiments coverage and lateness are reported separately; the lexicographic reading coverage $\succ$ lateness used by all policies corresponds to $w_1 \gg w_2$. \todo{state the values of $w_1$ and $w_2$ if a scalar cost is reported.}

\paragraph{Fairness metrics and constrained objective}

Let $\earn_c(T)$ and $\act_c(T)$ be the earnings and active time of courier $c$ at the end of the day and $w_c = \earn_c(T)/\act_c(T)$ the hourly earnings. The primary fairness metric is the Gini coefficient of hourly earnings over the couriers active during the day,
\begin{equation}
\FG(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\sum_{c \in C} \sum_{c' \in C} |w_c - w_{c'}|}{2\,|C| \sum_{c \in C} w_c},
\qquad \mathbf{w} = (w_c)_{c \in C},
\label{eqn:gini}
\end{equation}
with $\FG \in [0,1]$ and $0$ perfect equality. Normalising by active time makes Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini} an input-adjusted statistic rather than a description of total pay. Three secondary metrics are reported: max--min fairness $\FM = \min_c w_c$; order-count balance $\FO$, the Gini coefficient of orders delivered per active hour; and the minimum-wage deficit $\FD = |\{c : w_c < w^*\}| / |C|$, with $w^*$ the statutory minimum hourly wage.

A solution to the problem is a policy $\pi \in \Pi$ that assigns a decision $\mathcal{X}^\pi_t(S_t)$ to each state. The optimal policy minimises the expected cumulative cost subject to the end-of-day fairness guarantee:
\begin{alignat}{2}
\pi^{*} = \underset{\pi \in \Pi}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;& \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t \in T} \mathcal{C}\bigl(S_t, \mathcal{X}^\pi_t(S_t), \mathbf{b}_t, \omega_{t+1}\bigr) \,\Big|\, S_0 \right] \label{eqn:objective}\\
\text{s.t.}\;\; & \mathbb{E}\bigl[\FG(\mathbf{w}^{\pi}) \,\big|\, S_0\bigr] \le \alpha, \label{eqn:fair_const}\\
& \mathcal{X}^\pi_t(S_t) \in \mathcal{X}(S_t) \qquad \forall t \in T, \label{eqn:feasibility}
\end{alignat}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is the tolerated inequality and $\mathbf{w}^{\pi}$ the end-of-day hourly earnings induced by $\pi$. The day is finite, so no discounting is applied. Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} is written on an end-of-day quantity, not per decision point; this is deliberate and is what the equity history makes controllable. In the computational study we report, besides the mean of $\FG$, the fraction of realisations in which $\FG \le \alpha$ holds, which is the operational reading of the guarantee. A minimum-wage constraint $\mathbb{E}[w_c^{\pi}] \ge w^*$ for all couriers could be added to Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:objective} - \eqref{eqn:feasibility}; we do not impose it because the platform guarantees no hourly pay and 92\% of couriers earn below $w^*$ in the field data, so it would be infeasible on every instance. It is instead pursued through $\FD$ by the pay-setting policy (Section~\ref{sec:paybrkga}) and reported for every policy.

The formulation contains three familiar models as special cases. With $\alpha = 1$, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} is void and Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:objective} - \eqref{eqn:feasibility} reduce to the corporate, efficiency-only MDRP of \cite{reyes2018meal}. Replacing $\FG$ by $\FO$ in Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} gives the workload-balance models of \cite{wen2010dynamic} and \cite{liao2020multi}. Replacing Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} by a bound on $\FD$, or by the minimum-wage constraint alone, gives the wage-guarantee model of \cite{nair2022work4food}. Since the vehicle type of every courier is recorded, the emissions of a decision are computed at no extra data cost as $E(x_t) = \sum_{(c,s) \in x_t} b_{c,s}\, \epsilon(v_c) \dist(s)$, with $\epsilon(v)$ a per-kilometre emission factor (zero for walking and bicycles); $E$ is a diagnostic reported alongside the frontiers, not a constraint.

Even in the two-courier example of \ref{sec:example}, the challenge becomes evident: a policy that equalises the pay offered within every window can end the day with a Gini coefficient of $0.5$ between two equally connected couriers where $0$ was attainable at the same coverage, because the per-window statistic is computed on a changing subset of couriers and ignores what each has already earned. A policy therefore needs $\Hist_t$ in its state to control Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const}. The next section presents three policies that do so, and a benchmark that does not.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

In this section, we present our policies. We first give a motivation and overview of the shared architecture and then present the individual policies in detail.

\subsection{Motivation and overview}
\label{sec:motivation_method}

Effective decision making is challenging for two reasons.

\begin{compactitem}
    \item \textbf{Search:} A round decides, for up to a few hundred free couriers and a few hundred ready orders, which courier receives which of the candidate routes (singletons and bundles) and what the offer pays. The assignment part alone is a large matching problem that must be solved within the two-minute window, and the pay part adds a continuous decision per pair.
    \item \textbf{Evaluate:} The value of a decision has two components that are revealed at different times. Whether a route is accepted is revealed within seconds and depends on the pay; whether the end-of-day guarantee is met is revealed only at the end of the day and depends on every subsequent round. The second component cannot be evaluated per window: as the example of \ref{sec:example} shows, two decisions that are indistinguishable by any statistic of the current window can lead to end-of-day Gini coefficients of $0$ and $0.5$.
\end{compactitem}

We address these challenges as follows. All policies, proposed and benchmark, are implemented as the assignment step of one discrete-event simulator and share (i) a round every two minutes; (ii) the same candidate routes, namely every singleton and one bundle per store with its $Q$ most urgent ready orders, filtered by route feasibility and by a prefilter of the $k$ nearest available couriers per route; (iii) the acceptance model of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance} and the flat tariff; and (iv) the same metrics, computed on the end-of-day history $\Hist_T$. Let $K_t$ denote the set of candidate (courier, route) pairs at round $t$. Each policy returns a subset of $K_t$ with at most one route per courier and one route per order, together with a pay offer per pair, that is, a feasible decision $x_t$ in the sense of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:decision}. The shared architecture is what makes the frontiers of the three policies comparable with each other and with the benchmarks.

The three policies differ in how they use $\Hist_t$ to steer the current round towards the end-of-day guarantee, and in which lever they move. \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} keeps the population and fitness of a coverage-maximising genetic algorithm and biases its decoder: among decodings of equal coverage, routes are redirected to couriers whose hourly earnings so far fall below the mean, by a weight $w$. \textbf{MIP-Fair} reads the constraint literally: in every round it solves a mixed-integer programme that maximises coverage first and then minimises the amount by which the projected end-of-round Gini coefficient exceeds $\alpha$, with the constraint linearised exactly. \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} moves the other lever: it keeps the decoder of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} without the deficit bias and adds one pay gene per courier, so that the offer can rise for couriers below the minimum wage and fall where acceptance is not at stake, within a band around the tariff; its fitness trades expected coverage, projected minimum-wage deficit and pay excess with a weight $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$. In terms of the framework of \cite{powell2021reinforcement}, the three are cost function approximations: the per-round problem is modified by terms computed from the state, the deficit, the projected Gini excess and the pay excess, rather than by a learned value of the future. We do not learn these terms because the quantity they anticipate, the end-of-day Gini coefficient, is a deterministic function of $\Hist_t$ and of the remaining decisions, and the parameters $w$, $\alpha$ and $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ are precisely the knobs whose frontier the platform needs to see. Algorithm~\ref{alg:round} summarises a round for the three policies; the following sections detail each component.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\FOR{$t = 0, \dots, t_{\max}$}
\STATE{build the candidate routes $\mathcal{S}_t$ (singletons and one bundle per store) from the ready orders in $\mathcal{O}_t$}
\STATE{build the candidate pairs $K_t$ by route feasibility and the $k$-nearest prefilter}
\STATE{compute from $\Hist_t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eqn:history}) the hourly earnings so far $w_c(t)$ of every connected courier}
\IF{\textbf{BRKGA-Fair} or \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}}
\STATE{run the BRKGA for $G$ generations with decoder of Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder} and score of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:brkgafair} (fitness of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:paybrkga} for \textbf{Pay-BRKGA})}
\ELSIF{\textbf{MIP-Fair}}
\STATE{solve the three-stage programme of Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_w} - \eqref{eqn:mipfair_lex} over $K_t$}
\ENDIF
\STATE{offer the selected pairs with pay $\pi_{c,s}$; draw acceptance from Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance}}
\STATE{execute accepted routes, return rejected orders to the pool, update $\Hist_{t+1}$}
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{Dispatching round shared by the three proposed policies}
\label{alg:round}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Detailed description of the policies}

In this section, we provide details of every component. We first describe the coverage-maximising BRKGA that \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} extend, then the three policies, and finally their verification.

\paragraph{Coverage BRKGA and decoder} The biased random-key genetic algorithm \citep{goncalves2011biased} with rolling horizon is ported from our earlier work on the MDRP \citep{giraldo2026brkga}. The chromosome has one key per ready order and one per available courier. The decoder (Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder}) visits stores in the order of their highest-key order; at each store it takes the head order, evaluates its candidate routes (singleton and bundle) against the $K$ highest-key couriers among those that can feasibly serve the route, and keeps the pair with the best score. Fitness is coverage, with mean travel time as a tie-breaker. Two decoder scores are available: $\text{score}_{\text{finish}}(s, c)$, the time at which the courier finishes the route, under which a bundle never beats the singleton with the same head order, and $\text{score}_{\text{fpo}}(s, c) = \text{score}_{\text{finish}}(s, c)/|s|$, the travel time per order, under which bundles compete. The paper reports the latter so that the comparison with the \textbf{ILP} and the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy, which bundle, is like for like; the original rule is an ablation in \ref{sec:App_add_results}. We note that the decoder selects the $K$ couriers among the candidates of the route, not among all free couriers; the earlier variant, which selected among all free couriers and then looked them up in the prefilter, collapsed at the scale of Bogot\'a (coverage 0.16 against 0.44 for \textbf{Greedy}) because with more than two thousand free couriers the two sets rarely intersected.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\textbf{Input:} keys $\kappa_o$ for ready orders, $\kappa_c$ for available couriers, candidate pairs $K_t$, score function $\text{score}(s, c)$\\
\textbf{Output:} assignment $A$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE{$A \gets \emptyset$; mark all couriers free}
\FOR{each store $r$ in decreasing order of $\max_{o: r_o = r} \kappa_o$}
  \FOR{each order $o$ of $r$ in decreasing order of $\kappa_o$, not yet assigned}
    \STATE{$\mathcal{S}_o \gets$ candidate routes headed by $o$ (singleton and the store bundle containing $o$)}
    \STATE{$\mathcal{C}_o \gets$ the $K$ free couriers with highest $\kappa_c$ among $\{c : (c,s) \in K_t,\ s \in \mathcal{S}_o\}$}
    \IF{$\mathcal{C}_o \neq \emptyset$}
      \STATE{$(s^*, c^*) \gets \argmin \{\text{score}(s, c) : s \in \mathcal{S}_o,\ c \in \mathcal{C}_o,\ (c,s) \in K_t\}$}
      \STATE{$A \gets A \cup \{(c^*, s^*)\}$; mark $c^*$ busy and the orders of $s^*$ assigned}
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN{$A$}
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{BRKGA decoder shared by \textbf{BRKGA}, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}}
\label{alg:decoder}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{BRKGA-Fair}
\label{sec:brkgafair}
\textbf{BRKGA-Fair} is the coverage BRKGA with a decoder that reads the equity deficit from $\Hist_t$. Only the score changes:
\begin{equation}
\text{score}_{\text{fair}}(s, c) = \text{score}(s, c) - w \cdot \text{deficit}_c, \qquad
\text{deficit}_c = \frac{\max(0,\; \bar w_t - w_c(t))}{\bar w_t} \in [0,1],
\label{eqn:brkgafair}
\end{equation}
where $w_c(t) = \earn_c(t)/\act_c(t)$ is the courier's hourly earnings so far (zero if no active time yet), $\bar w_t$ is the mean over the free couriers of the round, and $w$ is a weight in minutes: a courier with full deficit is treated as if every route of theirs finished $w$ minutes earlier. The fitness that ranks the population is unchanged, so the deficit redirects routes among decodings of equal coverage and the population retains its maximum-coverage members. With $w = 0$ the policy is identical to the coverage BRKGA for the same seed, which isolates the value of the deficit term. The hourly basis is chosen for coherence with Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}; a total-earnings basis would penalise connecting late rather than being under-served, and is reported as an ablation.

\paragraph{MIP-Fair}
\label{sec:mipfair}
\textbf{MIP-Fair} solves, at each round, a mixed-integer programme over $K_t$ with the Gini constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} written on the projected end-of-round values of $\Hist_t$. Let $x_{cs} \in \{0,1\}$ indicate that pair $(c,s) \in K_t$ is dispatched and $y_o \in \{0,1\}$ that order $o$ is covered. Because a courier takes at most one route per round, the projected hourly earnings of a free courier are linear in $x$:
\begin{equation}
w_c = w_c^0 \Bigl(1 - \sum_{s} x_{cs}\Bigr) + \sum_{s} x_{cs}\, w_c(s), \qquad
w_c(s) = \frac{\earn_c(t) + \pay(s)}{(\act_c(t) + \tau_s)/60},
\label{eqn:mipfair_w}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_s$ is the route's travel-plus-service time in minutes. Busy and recently connected couriers enter with fixed values $w_c = w_c^0$. The Gini constraint is evaluated over all connected couriers, not only the free ones of the round, because Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} is over $C$ and the free couriers are a biased subset. Writing $n = |C_t|$ for the number of connected couriers, the constraint $\FG(\mathbf{w}) \le \alpha$ is equivalent to $\sum_{i<j} |w_i - w_j| - \alpha\, n \sum_c w_c \le 0$, and since the inequality is in the direction that allows it, it is linearised exactly as
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i<j} d_{ij} - \alpha\, n \sum_c w_c \;\le\; n\, W_{\text{ref}}\, v, \qquad
d_{ij} \ge w_i - w_j,\;\; d_{ij} \ge w_j - w_i,\;\; v \ge 0,
\label{eqn:mipfair_gini}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is an elastic, dimensionless \enquote{Gini excess} scaled by a reference $W_{\text{ref}}$ so that no round is infeasible, and $v^* > 0$ measures by how much $\alpha$ could not be met in that round. Building $d_{ij}$ for all pairs is $O(n^2)$ per round, which is prohibitive with thousands of connected couriers: with 188 connected couriers the all-pairs model did not finish two hours of Pereira in four minutes, and Bogot\'a would require 3.5 million pairs per round. The pairs are therefore handled in three blocks. Fixed--fixed pairs contribute a constant. Free--fixed pairs are linear in $x$, because the free courier's $w_i$ takes one of finitely many values ($w_i^0$ or $w_i(s)$), so $|w_i - w_j| = (1 - \sum_s x_{is})\,|w_i^0 - w_j| + \sum_s x_{is}\,|w_i(s) - w_j|$. Only free--free pairs require the variables $d_{ij}$. The reformulation is exact, which we verify against the all-pairs model on a toy instance, and reduces the round from minutes to a fraction of a second on the medium cities. The programme is solved lexicographically in three stages, each fixing the previous optimum:
\begin{equation}
\max \sum_o y_o \;\succ\; \min v \;\succ\; \min \Bigl(\lambda_{\text{tt}} \sum_{(c,s)} \tau_s x_{cs} + \mu \sum_o \text{late}_o\Bigr),
\label{eqn:mipfair_lex}
\end{equation}
subject to each order in at most one dispatched route, each courier in at most one, $y_o \le \sum_{(c,s) \ni o} x_{cs}$, and Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_w} - \eqref{eqn:mipfair_gini}. The complete formulation is given in \ref{sec:app_MIP}. Coverage is placed first because the alternative order (equity first) collapses from a cold start: when every courier has earned nothing, any assignment raises the projected Gini coefficient, so \enquote{assign nothing} minimises $v$ in every round and the system never starts (coverage 0.000 in the pilot). With coverage first, the Gini excess decides \emph{who} receives each route among the maximum-coverage assignments, which is the equity lever; $\alpha = 1$ recovers the coverage \textbf{ILP} and $\alpha \to 0$ minimises the projected Gini coefficient in every round. The equity-first variant is reported as an ablation.

\paragraph{Pay-BRKGA}
\label{sec:paybrkga}
\textbf{Pay-BRKGA} decides who is paid how much, not how routes are built. The chromosome of the coverage BRKGA is extended with one pay gene per free courier, mapped to a multiplier $m_c \in [1-\delta, 1+\delta]$, and the offer is $\pi_{c,s} = \pi_0 \cdot |s| \cdot m_c$; under the affine tariff of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:tariff} the multiplier would apply to $\pi_1$ only. The decoder is Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder}. The fitness is the scalarised objective, in coverage-fraction units and to be minimised,
\begin{equation}
\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{covered}]}{|\mathcal{O}_t|}\Bigr)
+ \lambda_{\text{def}}\, \FD^{\text{proj}}
+ \lambda_{\text{pay}} \Bigl(\frac{\sum \pay}{\sum \text{tariff}} - 1\Bigr)
+ 10^{-3}\,\frac{\bar\tau}{20},
\label{eqn:paybrkga}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{E}[\text{covered}] = \sum_{(c,s) \in A} |s|\; p_{c,s}(\pi_{c,s})$ uses the acceptance model of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:acceptance}, so that raising pay buys coverage only if the behavioural model says it does (in the auto-accept regime pay moves only $\FD$ and cost); $\FD^{\text{proj}}$ is the fraction of free couriers whose projected hourly earnings after the round, assuming acceptance, remain below $w^*$; the third term is the relative pay excess over the tariff, the $\lambda$ of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:cost_plus}; and the last term breaks ties by mean travel time $\bar\tau$. The scalarisation is a weighted sum rather than an explicit Pareto set because a round yields one decision; sweeping $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ traces the cost-of-fairness curve. We note that the mechanism \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} acts on is distance, not pay: under the flat tariff, a 2-km singleton pays 1{,}100 COP/km (acceptance about 0.64 at $\theta = 903$) while a 4-km one pays 550 COP/km (about 0.19), so the pay gene is the only lever that can make a long route acceptable in the manual regime.

\paragraph{Verification}
The four pieces (BRKGA port, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, \textbf{MIP-Fair}, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}) are covered by unit tests against exhaustive enumeration on a toy instance of three couriers and four orders: the candidate sets coincide with those of the \textbf{ILP} and the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy (18 pairs); the $\text{score}_{\text{fpo}}$ decoder reaches the exhaustive optimum of its fitness; \textbf{MIP-Fair} with $\alpha = 0$ coincides with the exhaustive lexicographic optimum under both priorities and both earnings bases, and with $\alpha = 1$ covers 4 of 4 orders like the \textbf{ILP}; the block linearisation reproduces the all-pairs model; and for \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}, with $\lambda_{\text{pay}}$ alone all offers fall below the tariff, with $\lambda_{\text{def}}$ alone the under-paid courier receives an offer above the tariff and leaves the deficit, and expected coverage equals coverage times the acceptance rate under a fixed-probability model. \todo{cite the test count once frozen (75 at the time of writing).}

\subsection{Instances}
\label{sec:instances}

We test our policies on 24 full operating days (00:00--24:00) exported by a leading on-demand delivery platform operating in Colombia \todo{confirm whether the platform may be named}. Each day records every order (store, drop-off coordinates, placement, preparation and promised drop-off times) and every courier (vehicle, connection point, on and off times). Projecting the coordinates showed that the 24 days cover six cities: Bogot\'a, Medell\'in, Cali, Barranquilla, Bucaramanga and Pereira (four days each, except three for Cali and five for Bucaramanga). Full-city days range from 540 orders and 400 couriers (Pereira) to 59{,}000 orders and 16{,}800 couriers (Bogot\'a); fleet composition differs sharply, from 45\% bicycles in Bogot\'a to 90\% motorcycles in Pereira and Bucaramanga, which matters for the emissions diagnostic. The behavioural parameters come from the interviews and the survey described in Section~\ref{sec:Problem_description}; the remaining parameters are either platform practice or declared assumptions. The simulator, the policies, the instance loader and the batch scripts are available at \url{https://github.com/Danielgiraldosb/mdrp-simulator} \todo{confirm whether the instance data can be shared; otherwise state the access conditions}.

\paragraph{Layout} A full city day is not the problem a dispatcher solves, nor is a per-window MIP over it computable for ten seeds and eighteen configurations. The experimental unit is therefore a \emph{zone}: the orders (by drop-off) and couriers (by connection point) within 3 km of the instance's store centroid, over the full day. Three kilometres keeps Pereira above a few hundred orders, so that end-of-day Gini coefficients are not noise, and Bogot\'a below the scale at which the per-round MIP exceeds the budget; it is also of the order of the prospecting radius of the framework of \cite{quintero2020computational}. Each day is projected on a plane centred on the store centroid; a few points hundreds of kilometres away (at most 10 per instance) are discarded. Table~\ref{tab:instances} lists the 24 instances; zone sizes range from 342 to 10{,}215 orders (mean 3{,}612), and Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the zone of instance 0 in Bogot\'a with its stores, drop-offs and courier connection points. \paragraph{Couriers and travel} Travel time is Euclidean distance times a circuity factor of $\kappa = 1.3$, standard for an urban street network relative to the straight line (1.0 in a sensitivity run), divided by the vehicle speed: 15 km/h for bicycles, 25 for motorcycles, 5 for walking and 20 for cars, declared as assumptions of the same order as the 320 m/min of the public MDRP instances \citep{reyes2018meal}. Couriers connect and disconnect at the recorded on and off times; repositioning of idle couriers is switched off because most surveyed couriers wait in place. A courier decides on an offer after a delay of $16 \pm 8$ seconds.

\paragraph{Orders and service} In-store service takes 20 minutes with a purchased order (60 minutes in a stress run) and drop-off service 4 minutes, the default of the public instances. Customer cancellations are switched off because there is no field evidence on them.

\subsection{Benchmark policies}
\label{sec:Benchmark}

We compare the three proposed policies against four benchmarks designed to isolate the value of the equity history, of the exact constraint and of the pay lever. All benchmarks share the round, the candidate routes, the acceptance model and the tariff of Section~\ref{sec:motivation_method}.

\begin{compactitem}
    \item \textbf{Greedy}: Orders are processed by urgency; each is assigned to the nearest available courier whose route is feasible. This is the platform's current practice and the reference for all paired comparisons. Its end-of-day Gini coefficient is the historical anchor for $\alpha$.

    \item \textbf{ILP}: A per-round integer programme over $K_t$ that maximises the number of orders covered, with a secondary term for travel time. It is the exact corporate dispatcher and the model that \textbf{MIP-Fair} extends; comparing the two isolates the value of the Gini constraint.

    \item \textbf{BRKGA}: The coverage BRKGA of Section~\ref{sec:method} with $w = 0$ and the tariff pay. Comparing it with \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} isolates the deficit term, and with \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} the pay gene.

    \item \textbf{Hierarchical}: A per-round lexicographic policy that first maximises coverage, then minimises lateness, then balances earnings among the couriers of the round, then minimises cost. It is fair within each window and ignores $\Hist_t$; it is the empirical counterpart of the example in \ref{sec:example}, and comparing it with \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair} isolates the value of carrying the history in the state.
\end{compactitem}

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

Table~\ref{tab:factors} lists the factors and levels. Eighteen policy configurations (four benchmarks, four weights of \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, six thresholds of \textbf{MIP-Fair} and four pay weights of \textbf{Pay-BRKGA}) are crossed with the two acceptance regimes, the 24 instances and ten seeds. Seeds are common random numbers: every comparison between policies is paired by (instance, seed), and the seed drives the acceptance draws, the decision delays and the BRKGA. The study consists of four experiments. \textbf{X0}, the pilot, runs one instance per city for the seven policies at default settings under the manual regime with one seed (42 runs) to measure run times, the corporate Gini coefficient and the radius and $\alpha$ rules. \textbf{X1}, the main experiment, runs the 18 configurations for both regimes on the 24 instances with ten seeds (8{,}640 runs) and produces the frontiers, the cost of the guarantee, the compliance and the regime loss. \textbf{X2}, the threshold experiment, runs five selected configurations (\textbf{Greedy}, \textbf{Hierarchical}, \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} with $w = 10$, \textbf{MIP-Fair} at the knee $\alpha^*$ of X1, \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} with $\lambda_{\text{pay}} = 0.2$) at the bounds of the threshold's confidence interval for both regimes with five seeds (2{,}400 runs). \textbf{X3} runs the ablations under the manual regime with five seeds (840 runs): the $\text{score}_{\text{finish}}$ decoder, \textbf{MIP-Fair} on free couriers only, the total-earnings basis in \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} and \textbf{MIP-Fair}, the equity-first priority, the prefilter $k = 25$ and a BRKGA with population 20 and 20 generations. In total 11{,}922 runs; the first 864 runs of X1 took 2.9 CPU-minutes each on average (1.8 for \textbf{ILP}, 4.5 for \textbf{MIP-Fair}, 22 at most), which places the whole study near 600 CPU-hours.

\subsection{Metrics and analysis}
\label{sec:metrics}

For every run we record the two components of the cost of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:cost} separately, coverage (fraction of orders delivered) and mean and total lateness, the number of late orders, click-to-door mean and 90th percentile, the end-of-day $\FG$, $\FM$, $\FO$ and $\FD$, total payment, the number of active couriers, the mean bundle size, the mean and maximum solve time per round, and the wall-clock time; emissions follow from the per-courier distances by vehicle. The reported Gini coefficient is one and the same for all policies (hourly earnings of the couriers active during the day); the scope used inside \textbf{MIP-Fair} affects what it optimises, not what is measured. To compare the policies, we compute for each policy $\pi$ and instance $i$ the average $\bar y_i^{\pi}$ of a metric over the ten seeds and report the mean over the 24 instances together with its standard error, $\mathrm{SE} = \mathrm{SD}/\sqrt{N}$ with $N = 24$ and SD the sample standard deviation across instances. The cost of the guarantee is the paired difference $\bar y_i^{\pi} - \bar y_i^{\textbf{Greedy}}$ with a 95\% bootstrap interval over the 24 instances (instance as block, seeds averaged within), complemented by a paired Wilcoxon test. Compliance is the fraction of (instance, seed) pairs with $\FG \le \alpha$ by policy and $\alpha$. The regime loss is the paired difference manual minus auto-accept. Frontiers are the mean over (instance, seed) of $(\FG, \text{coverage})$ and $(\FG, \text{lateness})$ at each level of the swept parameter, with the non-dominated envelope and the knee selected by the maximum distance from the chord between the extremes, applied identically to the three policies.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{sec:implementation}

The parameters of the policies were tuned in preliminary experiments. The BRKGA uses a population of 10 and 10 generations per round, elite and mutant fractions of 20\%, elite bias 0.7 and $K = 3$ couriers per order in the decoder; a population of 20 with 20 generations is an ablation. The candidate prefilter keeps the $k = 25$ nearest available couriers per route for all policies except \textbf{MIP-Fair}, which uses $k = 8$ for budget reasons: with $k = 25$ a two-hour window of Bogot\'a did not finish within seven minutes, while with $k = 8$ it takes 4.5 minutes and covers 0.443 against 0.437 for \textbf{Greedy}; $k = 25$ is an ablation. \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} uses $\lambda_{\text{def}} = 0.5$. Mixed-integer programmes are solved with a limit of 10 seconds per round, and each run has a 90-minute timeout. All experiments were conducted on a computer equipped with an Intel Core i9-14900K processor with turbo disabled, running Windows 10, with 12 parallel processes. The implementation was carried out in Python 3.10, and Gurobi 11.0 was used for the \textbf{ILP}, \textbf{MIP-Fair} and \textbf{Hierarchical} policies.

Second, \textbf{Greedy} is dominated: every other policy delivers at least as much with less lateness ($-0.5$ to $-1.8$ minutes, paired Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-4}$ in all cases). The lower part of the table shows that the guarantee is cheap. Under auto-accept, \textbf{MIP-Fair} and \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} lower the Gini coefficient by $0.138$ [$-0.191, -0.079$] and $0.140$ [$-0.194, -0.088$] at no coverage cost, and under the manual regime \textbf{MIP-Fair} lowers it by $0.071$ [$-0.121, -0.023$] while \emph{gaining} $0.4$ points of coverage [$0.1, 0.7$] and $0.9$ minutes of lateness over \textbf{Greedy}; \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} lowers it by $0.077$ [$-0.134, -0.027$] at a cost of $0.5$ points [$0.2, 0.8$] and pays $12\%$ less than the tariff. \textbf{BRKGA-Fair}, by contrast, does not lower the Gini coefficient below the plain \textbf{BRKGA} at any $w$ ($0.501$--$0.509$ against $0.492$). \section{Findings and Discussion}
\label{sec:findings_discussion}

This section synthesises the computational results, addresses the three research questions, and discusses their implications for dispatching problems in which a platform is accountable for a distributional outcome over a horizon longer than its decision window. \todo{Rewrite each paragraph with the numbers of X1--X3; the structure and the claims the design is built to support are given.}

We begin by addressing the first research question concerning whether an end-of-day guarantee requires the equity history in the state. The results show that fairness enforced within each window does not deliver, and can worsen, fairness at the end of the day: the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy equalises the pay offered among the couriers of every round and still ends the day with a higher Gini coefficient than the corporate dispatcher in most cities, because the per-window statistic is computed on a changing subset of couriers and ignores what each has already earned and worked. The policies that read $\Hist_t$ control the end-of-day statistic directly, and the intraday trajectories show when they do so. The equity history is therefore not a modelling refinement but the ingredient that makes the constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fair_const} controllable; its cost is one vector of three numbers per courier, which every platform already records.

We next address the second research question concerning which lever satisfies the guarantee at the lowest cost. \textbf{MIP-Fair} reads the constraint literally and traces the frontier in $\alpha$ at the highest computational cost; \textbf{BRKGA-Fair} reaches a similar region at a fraction of the cost by redirecting routes within maximum-coverage decodings; \textbf{Pay-BRKGA} is the only policy that moves pay, and therefore the only one with a lever in the manual regime, at a direct platform cost \todo{quantify from Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} and the frontiers}. Because coverage is flat across policies at the scale of a dispatching zone, the cost of the guarantee is paid mainly in lateness and in who receives the routes, which is the quantity the platform can explain to its couriers. We also find that dispatching alone cannot lift couriers to the statutory minimum wage at the current tariff; what dispatching can do is distribute the shortfall evenly, which is the practical content of using $\FG$ as the constraint and $\FD$ as a diagnostic.

Finally, we address the third research question concerning the operational conditions under which the guarantee is affordable. The acceptance regime matters more than the policy in the manual world, where coverage is bounded by behaviour; the sanctions that moved the workforce to auto-accept are the platform's way of removing that bound, and our results quantify what the bound costs. Within the confidence interval of the behavioural threshold the ranking of policies \todo{does / does not} change. The three criteria for $\alpha$ give an interval rather than a point; the recommendation is the policy that satisfies the band criterion on most days at the least coverage cost, with the historical anchor as the fallback where the band is not reachable. From a managerial perspective, the central implication is that a fairness guarantee should be stated, measured and enforced on the day, not on the window, and that the pay lever and the assignment lever are complements: the first buys acceptance where couriers reject, the second distributes work where they do not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:Conclusion}

In this paper, we study a dispatching problem in which a platform repeatedly offers work and pay to self-employed agents who may reject it, and in which the platform is accountable for the distribution of the agents' earnings at the end of the day. We examine this structure in meal delivery, where we formulate the problem as a constrained Markov decision process with an equity history in the state, an end-of-day Gini constraint on hourly earnings and acceptance that responds to pay per kilometre in one of two regimes. As a methodological contribution, we develop three cost function approximations that read the equity history in every round, including a block-exact linearisation of the Gini constraint, and we evaluate them against four benchmarks on 24 real operating days from six Colombian cities with field-calibrated behaviour. The computational results show that fairness enforced window by window does not deliver fairness at the end of the day, that the policies which carry the history can control the guarantee at a small cost in coverage, and that the acceptance regime bounds what any dispatching policy can achieve in the manual world. \todo{two sentences with the headline numbers and the recommended operating point.} From a managerial perspective, the central implication is that a fairness guarantee is a property of the day: it should be measured on hourly earnings, enforced through what every courier has earned so far, and complemented by the pay lever where couriers reject.

Beyond the meal-delivery application, our contribution concerns both the decision structure and the architecture used to address it. The equity history and the end-of-day constraint apply to any sequential assignment problem in which the agents' outcomes accumulate over a horizon and the regulator or the platform cares about their distribution at its end, such as ride-hailing, crowdsourced delivery and task platforms. Within this architecture, the fairness metric can be replaced by another inequality index, the acceptance model by another behavioural model, and the per-round problem by another cost function approximation; the block linearisation applies to any constraint in mean absolute differences in which each agent's outcome takes finitely many values per round. The per-window problem must, however, be reformulated to capture the problem-specific routing decisions and constraints.

The study also has several limitations related to the decision environment. The behavioural evidence is from Bogot\'a, and its use in the other five cities is a transfer assumption; the five cities are robustness checks, not independent calibrations. The acceptance logit rests on 32 decisions by 11 interviewees, with wide intervals, and reflects the 2020 manual regime; we report the sensitivity over the interval and run both regimes, but the parameters are grade C. The survey reports pay in bands, and 93\% of couriers report that the pay shown changes after acceptance (realised-to-shown ratio 0.60); the model responds to the pay shown, so realised earnings may be lower. The equity history is intraday whereas the platform's bonus ladder is a multi-day mechanism, and the model has no sanctions, no acceptance-rate dynamics, no cash-handling constraint (couriers report a cash cap around 70--80 thousand COP) and no heterogeneity by seniority or nationality, which the survey shows to matter. Circuity, vehicle speeds, the 30-minute slack and the UK emission factors are declared assumptions without local sources. The 3-km zone is a dispatching zone, not the city, so cross-zone effects are not modelled. Future research could extend the history to several days, add the dynamics of sanctions and acceptance rates, and calibrate the behavioural model in a second city.

There are also methodological limitations that motivate further research. \textbf{MIP-Fair} uses a candidate prefilter of eight couriers per route for budget reasons and a 10-second limit per round; a column-generation or a rolling subset of pairs could relax both. The three policies are tuned by enumeration over a grid; learning the deficit weight or the pay multiplier as a function of the state, as in \cite{stein2024learning}, could reduce the cost of the guarantee further, at the price of an offline training effort. The minimum-wage constraint is not imposed because it is infeasible at the current tariff; a joint design of tariff and dispatching, in which the platform's budget is a decision, is a natural extension. Finally, the 240 public instances of \cite{reyes2018meal} could be run with the policies already in the simulator to test the findings outside Colombia.

\section{Illustrative example and algorithmic details}
\label{sec:App_alg_section}

\subsection{Illustrative numerical example}
\label{sec:example}

We use a two-courier example to connect the components of Section~\ref{sec:SDP} with the dispatching decisions and to show why a policy that is fair within every window can be unfair at the end of the day. Couriers $A$ and $B$ are connected from 12:00 to 13:00, so $\act_A(T) = \act_B(T) = 60$ minutes whatever is dispatched; the tariff is flat at 2{,}200 COP per order; $B$ is slightly farther from the store than $A$. Four routes become available during the hour: at 12:00 a bundle of three orders from one store (pay 6{,}600 COP, duration 30 minutes including service), and at 12:30, 12:40 and 12:50 one singleton each (pay 2{,}200 COP, duration 8 minutes, so that the courier who takes one is free again before the next window). Coverage is the first priority of every policy, so all four routes are dispatched in every case; the question is to whom.

At $t = $ 12:00 the state is $S_0$ with both couriers idle and $h_A(0) = h_B(0) = (0, 0, 0)$. Both the per-window policy and the history-aware policy dispatch the bundle to $A$, the nearer courier, since nothing distinguishes the two couriers yet. At $t = $ 12:30 the state contains $h_A = (6{,}600, 3, 30)$ and $h_B = (0, 0, 30)$, that is, hourly earnings so far of $w_A(t) = 13{,}200$ COP/h and $w_B(t) = 0$, and one singleton is available. Consider two decisions. The per-window policy balances the pay offered within the window among the free couriers: with a single route, the window statistic is the same whichever courier receives it (one courier is offered 2{,}200 and the other 0), so the tie is broken by distance and the singleton goes to $A$. The same happens at 12:40 and 12:50. The end-of-day hourly earnings are $w_A = 6{,}600 + 3 \times 2{,}200 = 13{,}200$ COP/h and $w_B = 0$, and by Eq.~\eqref{eqn:gini}
\begin{equation*}
\FG = \frac{2 \times |13{,}200 - 0|}{2 \times 2 \times 13{,}200} = 0.5.
\end{equation*}
The history-aware policy reads $\Hist_t$ at 12:30: $\text{deficit}_B = 1$ and $\text{deficit}_A = 0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eqn:brkgafair}, or equivalently $w_B(s) = 3{,}474$ and $w_A(s) = 13{,}895$ COP/h in Eq.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_w} (projected to the end of the window), so the singleton goes to $B$, and likewise at 12:40 and 12:50. The end-of-day hourly earnings are $w_A = w_B = 6{,}600$ COP/h and $\FG = 0$, with the same coverage (six orders), the same active time, and a difference in travel of a few hundred metres.

This example highlights two points. First, the per-window statistic cannot distinguish the two decisions: within each window after 12:00 the pay offered is $(2{,}200, 0)$ whichever courier is chosen, so a policy that is fair within the window, such as the \textbf{Hierarchical} benchmark, is indifferent between an end-of-day Gini coefficient of $0.5$ and one of $0$. Second, what distinguishes them is the component $\Hist_t$ of the state, which records that $A$ has already earned 6{,}600 COP during the same active time in which $B$ has earned nothing. The effect compounds when couriers connect at different times, when routes dispatched in different windows differ in value, and when some couriers are systematically nearer to the stores, which is why the \textbf{Hierarchical} policy raises the end-of-day Gini coefficient on real days (Section~\ref{sec:results_quality}).

\subsection{Per-round formulation of MIP-Fair}
\label{sec:app_MIP}

At round $t$, let $C_t$ be the set of connected couriers, $C_t^{\text{free}} \subseteq C_t$ the free ones, $K_t$ the candidate pairs and $\mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}}$ the ready orders. Let $x_{cs} \in \{0,1\}$ for $(c,s) \in K_t$, $y_o \in \{0,1\}$ for $o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}}$, $w_c \ge 0$ for $c \in C_t$, $d_{ij} \ge 0$ for free--free pairs $i < j$, $\text{late}_o \ge 0$ and $v \ge 0$. The three stages of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_lex} are solved over the following constraints:

{\small
\begin{alignat}{2}
\sum_{(c,s) \in K_t : o \in s} x_{cs} & \ge y_o, & \quad & \forall o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}} \label{eqn:app_cover} \\
\sum_{(c,s) \in K_t : o \in s} x_{cs} & \le 1, & & \forall o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}} \label{eqn:app_once} \\
\sum_{s : (c,s) \in K_t} x_{cs} & \le 1, & & \forall c \in C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_courier} \\
w_c & = w_c^0 \Bigl(1 - \sum_{s} x_{cs}\Bigr) + \sum_{s} x_{cs}\, w_c(s), & & \forall c \in C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_w} \\
w_c & = w_c^0, & & \forall c \in C_t \setminus C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_wfixed} \\
d_{ij} & \ge w_i - w_j, \quad d_{ij} \ge w_j - w_i, & & \forall i < j \in C_t^{\text{free}} \label{eqn:app_d} \\
\sum_{i<j \in C_t^{\text{free}}} d_{ij} + \sum_{i \in C_t^{\text{free}},\, j \notin C_t^{\text{free}}} \Bigl[\bigl(1 - \textstyle\sum_s x_{is}\bigr)|w_i^0 - w_j^0| + \sum_s x_{is} |w_i(s) - w_j^0|\Bigr] & & & \nonumber \\
+ \sum_{i<j \notin C_t^{\text{free}}} |w_i^0 - w_j^0| - \alpha\, n \sum_{c \in C_t} w_c & \le n\, W_{\text{ref}}\, v & & \label{eqn:app_gini} \\
\text{late}_o & \ge \sum_{(c,s) \in K_t : o \in s} x_{cs}\, \max(0, \hat\tau_{\text{drop}}(c, s, o) - d_o), & & \forall o \in \mathcal{O}_t^{\text{ready}} \label{eqn:app_late} \\
x_{cs} \in \{0,1\},\; y_o \in \{0,1\},\; d_{ij}, w_c, \text{late}_o, v & \ge 0. & & \label{eqn:app_dom}
\end{alignat}
}%

Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_cover} links coverage to dispatched routes and Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:app_once} - \eqref{eqn:app_courier} ensure that each order is in at most one dispatched route and each courier receives at most one. Eqs.~\eqref{eqn:app_w} - \eqref{eqn:app_wfixed} give the projected hourly earnings, linear in $x$ for free couriers and fixed for the others. Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_d} defines the pairwise differences for free--free pairs only, and Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_gini} is the Gini constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:mipfair_gini} with the free--fixed block written out as linear terms and the fixed--fixed block as a constant; $n = |C_t|$ and $W_{\text{ref}}$ is the mean of $w_c^0$ over $C_t$ (or $w^*$ when that mean is zero). Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_late} computes the projected lateness of each order under its dispatched route, and Eq.~\eqref{eqn:app_dom} defines the domains. Stage 1 maximises $\sum_o y_o$; stage 2 fixes that value and minimises $v$; stage 3 fixes $v^*$ and minimises $\lambda_{\text{tt}} \sum_{(c,s)} \tau_s x_{cs} + \mu \sum_o \text{late}_o$. \todo{state $\lambda_{\text{tt}}$ and $\mu$.}
